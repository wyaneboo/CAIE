ML Systems Design & Distributed Training

Everything before this topic assumed the model fits on one device. It doesn't. A 70B-parameter model needs over a terabyte of memory just to train — an 80 GB GPU holds 7% of that. This topic is the arithmetic and engineering of splitting work across hundreds of GPUs: where the bytes go, how numbers are represented, how machines talk to each other, and how to size a cluster and a budget. It is almost entirely back-of-envelope math — which makes it the most physics-like topic in Level II, and a favorite exam hunting ground because the calculations have exact answers.

Learning outcomes
  • Calculate training memory: parameters, gradients, optimizer states, activations — and what ZeRO stages shard.
  • Describe data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism, and choose among them for a given model and interconnect.
  • Explain mixed precision (FP16/BF16/FP8) and why loss scaling exists.
  • Explain gradient accumulation and activation checkpointing and their memory/compute tradeoffs.
  • Describe all-reduce / all-gather / reduce-scatter and interconnect constraints (NVLink vs InfiniBand).
  • Size a training run: FLOPs = 6ND, GPU-hours, wall-clock time, and dollars.

12.1 Where the memory goes — the 16 bytes/parameter rule

Inference needs one copy of the weights. Training with Adam needs four kinds of state, and the standard mixed-precision recipe costs about 16 bytes per parameter before a single activation is stored:

  • Weights in BF16 — 2 bytes/param (the copy used for fast matmuls).
  • Gradients in BF16 — 2 bytes/param.
  • Optimizer states in FP32 — 12 bytes/param: a master copy of the weights (4) plus Adam's first moment \(m\) (4) and second moment \(v\) (4). The master copy exists because thousands of tiny updates would vanish if accumulated at 2-byte precision.

So a 7B model needs \(16 \times 7\times10^9 = 112\) GB of state — it does not fit on one 80 GB GPU even at micro-batch 1. (Full FP32 training also lands on ~16 bytes: 4+4+8. Mixed precision buys speed and cheaper activations, not smaller optimizer state.)

On top of state come activations — every intermediate tensor the backward pass will need (Topic 5: backprop reuses forward results). For a transformer they scale as \(\text{seq} \times \text{batch} \times \text{hidden} \times \text{layers}\): unlike parameter state, you control them through batch size and sequence length. Historically attention also stored the \(s \times s\) score matrix (a term growing as \(s^2\)); FlashAttention (Topic 8) recomputes it in tiles, removing that term — which is why long-context training became feasible.

Two levers stretch a too-small GPU before you buy more of them:

  • Gradient accumulation: run \(k\) small micro-batches, summing gradients, then apply one optimizer step. Mathematically identical to a \(k\times\) larger batch; activation memory stays at micro-batch size; you pay in wall-clock time, not memory.
  • Activation checkpointing (recomputation): store only layer inputs during the forward pass, and recompute the interiors during backward. Cuts activation memory by roughly the per-layer interior factor at the cost of ≈ one extra forward pass (~33% more compute). Trading memory for compute is often the best deal in town, because memory is the binding constraint.
⚛ Physics bridge

Checkpointing is exactly the trick of a reversible simulation: don't store every intermediate state of the trajectory, store sparse snapshots and re-integrate between them when you need the in-betweens. And the accounting mindset here is calorimetry — every byte must appear in the budget or the run dies with an OOM (out-of-memory) at hour 30. Senior engineers write the memory budget before launching, the way you write the error budget before the experiment.

12.2 Mixed precision — FP16, BF16, FP8 and loss scaling

A floating-point number is scientific notation in binary: sign, exponent (dynamic range), mantissa (relative precision). The formats differ only in how they split the bits:

FormatBits (sign·exp·mantissa)Max ≈Smallest normal ≈Relative precision ≈
FP321 · 8 · 233.4×10³⁸1.2×10⁻³⁸10⁻⁷
TF32 (matmul mode)1 · 8 · 103.4×10³⁸1.2×10⁻³⁸10⁻³
FP161 · 5 · 1065,5046.1×10⁻⁵10⁻³
BF161 · 8 · 73.4×10³⁸1.2×10⁻³⁸4×10⁻³
FP8 E4M31 · 4 · 34481.6×10⁻²6×10⁻²
FP8 E5M21 · 5 · 257,3446.1×10⁻⁵1.3×10⁻¹

The story of the table: FP16 has a narrow exponent, so small gradients (typically 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁸) underflow to zero. The fix is loss scaling: multiply the loss by a factor \(S\) (say 2¹⁴) before backward — every gradient scales by \(S\) and slides up into representable range — then divide by \(S\) before the optimizer step. Dynamic loss scaling grows \(S\) until an overflow (inf/NaN) appears, then backs off. BF16 simply keeps FP32's 8 exponent bits and sacrifices mantissa: same range, coarser precision, no loss scaling needed — which is why it's the modern default, with the FP32 master weights absorbing the coarseness. FP8 (H100 era) pushes further: E4M3 for weights/activations in the forward pass, E5M2 (more range) for gradients, with per-tensor scaling factors maintained by the framework.

⚛ Physics bridge

Loss scaling is gain-staging on a lock-in amplifier: your signal sits below the ADC's least significant bit, so you amplify it into the instrument's dynamic range, measure, and divide the gain back out. Exponent bits are the instrument's dynamic range; mantissa bits are its resolution. BF16 vs FP16 is choosing a voltmeter with a wider scale but one fewer digit — the right call when the danger is clipping, not read noise. And gradient noise from SGD dwarfs 4×10⁻³ rounding noise anyway — that's why 7 mantissa bits are enough.

12.3 The parallelism menu — data, tensor, pipeline, ZeRO

Four ways to split training across \(n\) devices, distinguished by what is divided and what must be communicated:

  • Data parallelism (DP): replicate the model; split the batch. Each GPU computes gradients on its shard; an all-reduce averages them so every replica steps identically. Communication: one gradient-sized all-reduce per step. Requires the whole model (and its 16 bytes/param) to fit on each GPU.
  • ZeRO / FSDP: keep data parallelism, but stop replicating. Stage 1 shards optimizer states across the DP ranks; stage 2 also shards gradients; stage 3 (= PyTorch FSDP, Fully Sharded Data Parallel) shards the parameters themselves, all-gathering each layer's weights just-in-time for its forward/backward and discarding them after. Memory per GPU falls toward \(16\Psi/n\); communication rises from \(2\Psi\) to \(3\Psi\) per step (a 1.5× overhead for an n-fold memory cut — usually a spectacular trade).
  • Tensor parallelism (TP): split inside each layer — one giant matmul becomes \(n\) column- or row-slices computed on different GPUs, stitched together with an all-reduce of activations per layer, per micro-batch, forward and backward. Enormous communication volume at tiny intervals — viable only over intra-node NVLink. It's the only option when a single layer's working set outgrows a GPU.
  • Pipeline parallelism (PP): split by depth — GPU 0 gets layers 1–8, GPU 1 gets 9–16, … Communication is tiny (boundary activations, point-to-point), so PP crosses nodes happily. The cost is the bubble: stages idle while the pipeline fills and drains. With \(p\) stages and \(m\) micro-batches in flight, the idle fraction is \((p-1)/(m+p-1)\) — you fight it by feeding many micro-batches.

Real frontier runs compose them — "3D parallelism": TP inside each node (highest bandwidth link), PP across nodes to hold the model, DP/ZeRO across the remaining dimension for throughput. The design rule the exam wants: match each parallelism's communication pattern to the interconnect tier that can afford it.

⚛ Physics bridge

This is domain decomposition, straight from lattice simulation. Compute scales with the volume of each subdomain; communication scales with its surface. Parallel efficiency is a surface-to-volume argument: make subdomains too small (too many GPUs) and the surface term dominates — exactly the flattening you'll see in the cluster planner below. TP is a fine-grained decomposition (huge surface, needs the fastest links); PP is coarse slabs (small surface, tolerates slow links).

12.4 Communication — the primitives and the pipes

Three collective operations (from MPI, now NCCL) run distributed training: all-reduce — every GPU ends with the elementwise sum of everyone's tensor (DP gradient averaging); all-gather — every GPU ends with the concatenation of everyone's shard (ZeRO-3 materializing a layer); reduce-scatter — the sum, but each GPU keeps only its shard (the other half of ZeRO). The identity all-reduce = reduce-scatter + all-gather is why ZeRO's total traffic is only 1.5× plain DP's.

Ring all-reduce is the classic implementation: arrange \(n\) GPUs in a ring; each sends chunks around it. Every GPU transmits \(2\frac{n-1}{n}M \approx 2M\) bytes for an \(M\)-byte tensor — nearly independent of \(n\). Bandwidth needed doesn't grow with the cluster; latency (number of ring steps, \(2(n-1)\)) does, which is why tree and hierarchical variants exist for very large \(n\).

The pipes come in tiers, and the ~15× gaps between them dictate the whole design: NVLink within a node (~900 GB/s per H100), InfiniBand/RoCE between nodes (~50–100 GB/s), PCIe to the host (~64 GB/s). Hence the placement rule from §12.3, and one more senior-engineer habit: overlap communication with compute — start all-reducing layer \(k\)'s gradients while backprop is still working on layer \(k-1\). Frameworks do this automatically; knowing it exists explains why measured scaling can approach ideal despite nonzero communication.

12.5 Sizing a run — FLOPs, GPU-hours, dollars

The master formula: training a dense transformer with \(N\) parameters on \(D\) tokens costs $$C \approx 6ND \ \text{FLOPs}$$ (≈2 FLOPs per parameter per token forward — each parameter participates in one multiply-add — and the backward pass costs about twice the forward; checkpointing adds ~1 forward more). Divide by what your hardware actually delivers: $$\text{time} = \frac{6ND}{n_{\text{gpu}} \times \text{peak FLOP/s} \times \text{MFU}},$$ where MFU (model FLOPs utilization) is the fraction of peak actually spent on the model's math — 35–50% is good; the rest is communication, memory stalls, and bubbles.

Worked example (exam-style): 7B params × 1.4T tokens ⇒ \(6 \times 7{\times}10^9 \times 1.4{\times}10^{12} \approx 5.9\times10^{22}\) FLOPs. On H100s (990 TFLOP/s BF16) at 40% MFU ⇒ \(\approx 1.5\times10^{8}\) GPU-seconds ≈ 41,000 GPU-hours. On 256 GPUs: ~6.7 days; at $2.50/GPU-hr: ~$103k. Notice what the GPU count changed: the calendar, not the bill — GPU-hours are fixed by physics until communication overhead starts inflating them.

Jargon decoder
OOM
Out of memory — the CUDA error that ends a run; the whole topic is about avoiding it deliberately rather than by luck.
master weights
The FP32 copy of parameters kept by the optimizer so tiny updates don't round away in BF16.
micro-batch / global batch
What one GPU processes in one forward pass vs the total across accumulation steps and DP ranks: global = micro × accum × DP degree.
ZeRO stage 1/2/3
Shard optimizer states / + gradients / + parameters across data-parallel ranks. Stage 3 ≈ PyTorch FSDP.
all-reduce / all-gather / reduce-scatter
Everyone gets the sum / everyone gets all shards / everyone gets one shard of the sum.
NVLink / InfiniBand
Intra-node GPU fabric (~900 GB/s) vs inter-node network (~50–100 GB/s); the 15× gap drives parallelism placement.
pipeline bubble
Idle time while a pipeline fills/drains: fraction (p−1)/(m+p−1) for p stages, m micro-batches.
MFU
Model FLOPs utilization: useful model FLOPs ÷ peak hardware FLOPs; the honesty metric for training efficiency.
loss scaling
Multiplying the loss by S so FP16 gradients don't underflow; unscaled before the update. BF16 mostly obviates it.
NCCL
NVIDIA's collective-communication library ("nickel") implementing the primitives over NVLink/IB.

Self-check

1. You try to fine-tune a 7B model with Adam in mixed precision on a single 80 GB GPU (no ZeRO, no offload). Before any activations:

b. The 16 bytes/param rule. Batch size only controls activations, which aren't the problem here. Escape hatches: ZeRO/offload, LoRA (Topic 9 — freeze the base, train adapters, optimizer state shrinks ~1000×), or a smaller optimizer.

2. Training in FP16 diverges with zero gradients in early layers; the same run in BF16 works untouched. The mechanism:

c. Range, not precision — BF16 actually has fewer mantissa bits (7 vs 10). The FP16 fix is loss scaling; the BF16 fix is not needing one. Classic exam discriminator between the two formats.

3. A 70B model must train across 4 nodes (8 GPUs each; NVLink inside, InfiniBand between). Sound placement:

a. TP all-reduces activations every layer — it dies on the ~15×-slower inter-node link. PP sends only boundary activations, so it crosses nodes cheaply. And (c) fails arithmetic: 70B × 16 bytes = 1.12 TB of state per replica.

4. An engineer hits OOM at batch 32, and switches to micro-batch 8 with gradient accumulation 4. What changes?

d. Accumulated gradients sum to the same global-batch gradient (same update, up to batch-norm-style caveats). Optimizer states are untouched — they scale with parameters, not batch. The price is wall-clock time.
