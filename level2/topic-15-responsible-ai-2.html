Responsible AI II — Evaluation, Fairness & Interpretability

Level I asked whether a model is fair; Level II asks how you would know, in technical detail. This topic is the toolbox: attribution methods that assign credit to inputs, mechanistic interpretability that reads a model's internals, the adversarial attacks that expose how brittle "accuracy" can be, and the privacy techniques whose guarantees you must be able to state precisely. The unifying theme — and the reason each tool comes with a warning label — is that every one of these methods is an approximation, and using it as if it were ground truth is its own failure mode.

Learning outcomes
  • Apply SHAP and LIME; state their assumptions and limits; read attention analysis skeptically.
  • Describe mechanistic interpretability: features, circuits, probing, superposition.
  • Audit a model for subgroup bias and reason about remediation tradeoffs.
  • Describe adversarial attacks — evasion, poisoning, prompt injection, jailbreaks — and defenses.
  • Explain model cards and datasheets and what belongs in them.
  • Evaluate differential privacy and federated learning by their actual guarantees and costs.

15.1 Post-hoc attribution — SHAP and LIME

A trained model is a function \(f(x)\); attribution asks which inputs drove this particular prediction. Two dominant methods, both model-agnostic (they probe \(f\) from outside):

LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations): to explain one point, sample perturbations around it, label them with \(f\), and fit a simple linear model in that neighborhood, weighted toward the point. The linear coefficients are the explanation. It is a local Taylor expansion by sampling — trustworthy only within the neighborhood, and sensitive to how you sample and how big the neighborhood is.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) borrows the unique fair credit-allocation from cooperative game theory. Treat features as players and the prediction as a payout; a feature's Shapley value is its average marginal contribution over all orderings of adding features to the "coalition": $$\phi_i = \sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}\big[f(S\cup\{i\}) - f(S)\big].$$ Its defining property is local accuracy: the attributions sum exactly to the prediction minus a baseline, \(f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_i \phi_i\) — every unit of output is accounted for. The cost is combinatorial (\(2^{|F|}\) coalitions), so practical SHAP uses approximations (KernelSHAP, or exact TreeSHAP for tree ensembles — Topic 4).

⚛ Physics bridge

The Shapley value is a symmetry-and-conservation argument. Demand four axioms — efficiency (contributions sum to the total, a conservation law), symmetry (identical players get identical credit), null-player (a feature that never changes the output gets zero), and additivity — and a unique allocation is forced, exactly as demanding Lorentz invariance plus a few axioms forces the form of a physical law. The combinatorial average over feature orderings is a sum over microstates; SHAP is the ensemble average of a feature's marginal effect.

Interactive — attribution explorer (loan approval)
A transparent scoring model. Set an applicant, and watch SHAP values decompose the prediction as a waterfall from the population baseline — the bars sum exactly to the score. Compare with a LIME local-linear estimate.

Things to try: (1) The bars always land exactly on the final score — that's SHAP's local accuracy (efficiency axiom), and why it's audit-grade where LIME is only indicative. (2) Push credit score across ~640: its SHAP bar flips from red (pushing toward denial) to blue (toward approval) — attribution is local, so the same feature helps some applicants and hurts others. (3) Watch LIME's estimate track SHAP near typical inputs but drift at the extremes, where the local linear fit breaks — the neighborhood assumption failing in real time.

Both methods share a warning the exam wants stated: an explanation of a biased model is a faithful explanation of a biased model. Attribution tells you what the model did, never whether it should — and a plausible-looking explanation can lull reviewers into trusting a bad model (the "interpretability theater" critique).

15.2 Attention is not explanation; mechanistic interpretability

A tempting shortcut for transformers: read the attention weights and call the high-attention tokens the "explanation." Resist it. Attention weights are one factor multiplied by value vectors, competing with residual streams and later layers; you can often change the attention map substantially without changing the prediction, or hold the prediction while permuting attention. Attention is a component of the computation, not a certified account of it — the "Attention is not Explanation" literature is a favorite exam citation.

Mechanistic interpretability is the more ambitious program: reverse-engineer the learned algorithm from the weights, as you would decompile a binary. Its vocabulary:

  • Features: directions in activation space that represent human-meaningful concepts ("this text is in French," "the Golden Gate Bridge").
  • Circuits: connected subgraphs of attention heads and MLP neurons that implement a specific behavior — e.g., induction heads, which copy repeated sequences and underlie much of in-context learning.
  • Probing: train a small classifier on a layer's activations to test whether a property is linearly present — with the caveat that a probe can succeed because the probe learned the property, not because the model uses it.
  • Superposition: networks pack more features than they have neurons by encoding them as overlapping directions — which is why single neurons are usually polysemantic (firing for unrelated concepts), and why sparse autoencoders are used to tease the overlapping features apart.
⚛ Physics bridge

Superposition is an under-determined basis problem. With \(n\) neurons but \(m \gg n\) sparse features, the network stores them as near-orthogonal directions in an \(n\)-dimensional space — possible only because the features rarely co-activate (sparsity), the same reason compressed sensing recovers a sparse signal from far fewer measurements than dimensions. A sparse autoencoder is the recovery algorithm: find the overcomplete dictionary of directions whose sparse combinations reconstruct the activations — dictionary learning, straight out of signal processing.

15.3 Adversarial machine learning

Standard accuracy measures performance on natural inputs. Adversarial ML studies inputs chosen by an adversary, and the gap is alarming. The canonical result: a model with 99% test accuracy can be driven to near-0% by perturbations too small for a human to see. The attack taxonomy by when and how it strikes:

  • Evasion (test-time): perturb an input to flip the prediction. FGSM takes one step along the gradient of the loss with respect to the input: \(x' = x + \varepsilon\,\text{sign}(\nabla_x L)\). The genius and the horror is that it moves every pixel by a tiny \(\varepsilon\), and the \(d\) tiny nudges sum in high dimension to a large change in the model's score while barely denting the image.
  • Poisoning (train-time): inject crafted examples into the training set to install a backdoor (a trigger pattern that forces a chosen output) or degrade accuracy. The supply-chain risk of scraped data and third-party datasets.
  • Prompt injection (LLM-specific): untrusted content in the model's context (a web page, a retrieved document, an email) carries instructions the model follows as if they were the user's — the confused-deputy problem, and the central security issue of agentic systems (Topic 18). Jailbreaks are the adjacent class: prompts that bypass safety training.

Defenses are partial and costly. Adversarial training (train on attacked examples) is the strongest evasion defense and typically lowers clean accuracy — a real robustness/accuracy tradeoff, not a bug. Input sanitization, ensembles, and detection help but are routinely broken by adaptive attacks; gradient masking defenses that merely hide the gradient give false security. For prompt injection the durable pattern is architectural, not a filter: never let untrusted text carry privileges — separate data from instructions, sandbox tools, require confirmation for consequential actions (Topics 18 and 19).

Interactive — FGSM: how a tiny nudge flips a classifier
A linear classifier on a 400-pixel image (two classes). Increase the attack budget ε and watch the perturbation — invisibly small per pixel — accumulate across dimensions until the decision flips, while the image barely changes.

Things to try: (1) At ε just past the flip point, the two images look identical — that's the whole threat. (2) Slide dimensions from 16 to 784 at fixed ε: the same per-pixel budget flips the label far more easily in high dimension, because the score change grows like ε·d while the visible change per pixel stays ε. Adversarial fragility is largely a high-dimensional phenomenon. (3) The perturbation isn't noise — it's the sign of the gradient, a structured image aimed straight at the decision boundary.

15.4 Privacy — differential privacy and federated learning

Models memorize. Membership-inference and extraction attacks can recover whether — and sometimes what — a specific record was in the training set (a direct GDPR problem, Topic 7). Differential privacy (DP) is the rigorous defense, and its guarantee is worth stating exactly: a randomized algorithm \(M\) is \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP if for any two datasets differing in one record and any outcome set \(S\), $$P[M(D)\in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\,P[M(D')\in S] + \delta.$$ In words: any one person's presence changes the output distribution by at most a factor \(e^{\varepsilon}\) — so no output can be strong evidence that you were in the data. It is a worst-case, mathematically provable guarantee, not a heuristic. Achieved by adding calibrated noise (Laplace or Gaussian, scaled to the query's sensitivity — how much one record can move it); in deep learning, DP-SGD clips per-example gradients and adds Gaussian noise each step. Smaller \(\varepsilon\) = more privacy = more noise = lower accuracy: a dial you set, not a box you check. The composition theorem is the catch that trips people — privacy loss accumulates across queries/epochs, so you spend from a finite budget.

Federated learning (FL) answers a different question — data locality, not anonymity. Raw data never leaves the device; each client trains locally and sends only model updates, which a server aggregates (FedAvg). It reduces central data collection, but the updates themselves leak information (gradients can be inverted to reconstruct inputs), so FL is combined with DP and secure aggregation for a real privacy guarantee. The common exam trap: treating FL as automatically private. It is not — it is a data-governance architecture that needs DP bolted on to make a formal claim.

Interactive — the differential-privacy dial
Release a private statistic (mean income of a group) with Laplace noise calibrated to ε. See the privacy–utility tradeoff, and how a change in one person's record hides inside the noise as ε shrinks.

Things to try: (1) The two curves are the output distributions with and without one specific high earner in the group. At small ε they overlap almost completely — an attacker seeing the release can't tell which world they're in; that indistinguishability is the DP guarantee. (2) Raise ε toward 5: the curves separate and the noise shrinks — accurate release, weak privacy. There is no free lunch. (3) Grow n: the same person moves the true mean less, so the same privacy needs less noise — privacy is cheaper in crowds.

15.5 Documentation as accountability — model cards and datasheets

The last defense is written, not computed. Model cards (Mitchell et al.) document a model's intended use, out-of-scope uses, training data, and — crucially — disaggregated evaluation: performance broken out by subgroup, so a headline accuracy can't hide a failure on a minority (the audit of §15's opening, made routine). Datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al.) do the same upstream: motivation, collection process, consent, known biases, maintenance. Both exist because harms are usually knowable in advance and omitted only for lack of a required place to write them down — the AI analog of a materials safety data sheet. Under emerging regulation (Topic 7's EU AI Act) this documentation is moving from best practice to legal obligation.

Jargon decoder
SHAP / Shapley value
Game-theoretic feature attribution; contributions sum exactly to the prediction (local accuracy). TreeSHAP is the exact, fast version for tree models.
LIME
Local surrogate: fit a linear model on perturbations around one point; indicative, neighborhood-dependent.
circuit / induction head
A subnetwork implementing a behavior; induction heads copy repeated patterns and drive in-context learning.
superposition
More features than neurons, stored as overlapping directions; why neurons are polysemantic. Sparse autoencoders disentangle them.
probing
Training a classifier on activations to test if a property is present — but probe success ≠ model use.
FGSM / evasion
One-step gradient attack that flips a test-time prediction with an imperceptible perturbation.
poisoning / backdoor
Corrupting training data to degrade accuracy or install a trigger→target behavior.
prompt injection
Untrusted context text hijacking an LLM's instructions — the confused-deputy attack of agentic systems.
(ε,δ)-differential privacy
One record changes the output distribution by at most e^ε (plus slack δ); smaller ε = more noise = more privacy.
DP-SGD
Differentially private training: clip per-example gradients, add Gaussian noise, track the spent privacy budget.
federated learning
Train on-device, share updates not data; a locality architecture, private only when combined with DP + secure aggregation.
model card / datasheet
Standardized documentation of a model's / dataset's intended use, data, and disaggregated performance and limits.

Self-check

1. A regulator asks you to prove that your explanation attributes credit consistently and that the attributions fully account for each decision. Between LIME and SHAP:

c. Local accuracy/efficiency is precisely the "fully account for" property; symmetry and consistency are the others. LIME's local linear fit has no such guarantee — its coefficients don't sum to the output and shift with the sampling neighborhood. Both still explain the model, not its correctness.

2. An engineer says "the model attends 80% to the word 'refund', so that word explains the classification." The correct pushback:

b. The "Attention is not Explanation" result. Attention interacts with value vectors, residual streams, and downstream layers; correlation with importance is weak and manipulable. Use it as a hint, corroborate with attribution or ablation.

3. Why are imperceptible adversarial perturbations so effective on high-dimensional inputs?

d. FGSM's linear view: the dot product of the weight vector with an ε·sign(gradient) perturbation grows with dimension. The visible change stays ε per pixel while the decision-relevant change scales with d — which the widget lets you watch directly.

4. A vendor pitches federated learning as making your system "automatically GDPR-compliant because raw data never leaves the user's phone." The accurate assessment:

a. The standard trap. FL reduces central collection but leaks through shared gradients (gradient-inversion attacks reconstruct training images). Differential privacy is the mechanism with the provable bound; FL and DP are complementary, not substitutes.
