Advanced Architectures: Transformers, Diffusion, GNNs

Advanced architectures are structured answers to structured data: attention routes information across a sequence, latent-variable and diffusion models represent uncertainty in generation, and message passing respects graph connectivity. The goal is not to memorize brand names; it is to track tensor shapes, information flow, compute, memory, and failure modes well enough to choose a defensible design.

Learning outcomes
  • Derive scaled dot-product attention and explain the √d factor; decompose multi-head attention into Q/K/V projections and justify multiple heads.
  • Explain positional encodings (sinusoidal, learned, RoPE, ALiBi) and their extrapolation properties; describe the full transformer block and pre-LN vs post-LN.
  • Analyze attention's quadratic complexity; describe FlashAttention, sparse/linear/sliding-window variants; explain KV caching and MQA/GQA/paged attention.
  • Describe VAEs (reparameterization trick), GANs (minimax, mode collapse), diffusion models (forward/reverse, DDPM vs DDIM); compare diffusion vs autoregressive generation.
  • Describe GNN message passing and CLIP-style contrastive alignment.

Three distinctions that prevent category errors

Training versus inference: parallel training can coexist with serial decoding. Exact versus approximate: FlashAttention reorganizes exact softmax attention up to floating-point ordering, while sparse or kernel variants change the operator. Architecture versus objective: a transformer can be encoder-only, decoder-only, or part of a diffusion network depending on its mask and loss.

8.1 Attention, derived

Purpose and tensor shapes — route information by content

Attention lets each query position form a weighted mixture of value vectors. With \(X\in\mathbb R^{B\times n\times d_{model}}\), learned projections produce queries, keys, and values. In one self-attention head, \(Q=XW_Q\), \(K=XW_K\), and \(V=XW_V\), with \(W_Q,W_K\in\mathbb R^{d_{model}\times d_k}\) and \(W_V\in\mathbb R^{d_{model}\times d_v}\). The score matrix compares every query with every key:

$$\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}_{keys}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}+M\right)V.$$

Softmax runs across the key axis for each query row. Mask \(M\) adds zero where attention is allowed and a sufficiently negative value where it is forbidden. The result has one \(d_v\)-wide vector per query. Cross-attention uses queries from one sequence and keys/values from another, so query and key lengths need not match.

Shape ledger

For batch \(B=2\), four queries, six keys, and one head with \(d_k=d_v=64\): \(Q:[2,4,64]\), \(K,V:[2,6,64]\), scores \(QK^\top:[2,4,6]\), and output \([2,4,64]\). The 48 score cells are per head; eight heads create 384 cells.

Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)? — control logit scale

Suppose components \(q_j,k_j\) are independent, zero-mean, and unit-variance. Then \(q\cdot k=\sum_{j=1}^{d_k}q_jk_j\) has variance \(d_k\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{d_k}\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) makes the logit variance approximately one under those assumptions. Without scaling, increasing \(d_k\) tends to produce more extreme softmax probabilities and smaller gradients for non-winning entries. Learned projections and normalization mean the assumptions are approximate; the factor is scale control, not a guarantee of ideal entropy.

Worked two-key attention

Let \(q=[1,0]\), \(k_1=[1,0]\), \(k_2=[0,1]\), so scaled logits are \([1/\sqrt2,0]\approx[0.707,0]\). Stable softmax gives weights about \([0.670,0.330]\). With \(v_1=[2,0]\), \(v_2=[0,4]\), the output is \(0.670v_1+0.330v_2\approx[1.340,1.320]\). Attention mixes values; it does not copy the largest value elementwise.

Masks, stable softmax, and an inspectable attention map

A causal decoder forbids key positions later than the query; padding masks exclude artificial tokens; structured masks implement local or block-sparse patterns. Compute softmax by subtracting each row maximum before exponentiation. A fully masked row has no valid probability distribution and must be prevented explicitly—otherwise implementations can emit NaN values. 

ATTEND(Q, K, V, allowed):
  logits = MATMUL(Q, TRANSPOSE_LAST_TWO(K)) / SQRT(d_key)
  for each query row:
      require at least one allowed key
      set forbidden logits to NEGATIVE_INFINITY
      probabilities = STABLE_SOFTMAX(logits across key axis)
  return MATMUL(probabilities, V)

Low entropy means concentrated weights, not automatically better attention. The unscaled toy becomes sharper with width because its logits are constructed with the expected \(\sqrt{d_k}\) growth; real heads have learned, correlated activations.

Multi-head attention — parallel learned subspaces with explicit dimensions

Multi-head attention projects into \(h\) sets of Q/K/V tensors, applies attention independently, concatenates head outputs, and multiplies by \(W_O\). If \(hd_k=hd_v=d_{model}\), the four dense projections contain about \(4d_{model}^2\) weights, excluding biases. Multiple heads permit different learned projections and attention distributions; they can specialize, overlap, or become redundant. Semantic roles are observed behaviors, not guaranteed by the architecture.

Position information — definition beyond training length is not successful extrapolation

Content-only self-attention is permutation equivariant, so position must enter through embeddings, biases, rotations, masks, or recurrence. A method being mathematically defined at a longer length does not mean a model trained on short sequences will use that length reliably; data, optimization, numerical precision, and attention pattern all matter.

Physics bridge — phase and temperature

RoPE treats dimension pairs like complex amplitudes rotated by position; relative phase affects their inner product. Sinusoidal features are a Fourier basis over position. Scaled attention resembles a Boltzmann distribution over keys, but its logits are learned compatibilities rather than physical energies.

The full transformer block — attention routes, FFN transforms

A common pre-normalized block is \(u=x+\mathrm{MHA}(\mathrm{Norm}(x))\), followed by \(y=u+\mathrm{FFN}(\mathrm{Norm}(u))\). Attention mixes positions; the FFN applies the same nonlinear channel transformation independently at each position; residual connections provide an identity path. LayerNorm or RMSNorm and gated FFNs are implementation choices, not different sequence objectives.

PlacementSketchPractical trade-off
pre-LN\(x+F(\mathrm{LN}(x))\)often easier to optimize at depth because the residual identity path avoids repeated post-add normalization; final normalization is commonly used
post-LN\(\mathrm{LN}(x+F(x))\)the original transformer placement; can be more sensitive to initialization, depth, and warmup, though successful variants exist
Block parameter estimate

For \(d_{model}=768\) and a standard FFN width 3072, attention projections use \(4(768)^2=2{,}359{,}296\) weights. Two FFN matrices use \(2(768)(3072)=4{,}718{,}592\). Total is 7,077,888 before biases and normalization. A gated FFN has a different three-matrix count and often adjusts hidden width to control parameters.

Quadratic attention and efficiency methods — identify the bottleneck first

Full attention forms \(n_qn_k\) scores per head. Self-attention therefore costs roughly \(O(Bhn^2d_k)\) arithmetic and a naive implementation stores \(O(Bhn^2)\) score/probability intermediates. At \(n=131{,}072\), one \(n\times n\) matrix contains about 17.2 billion entries before multiplying by heads, batches, or layers.

MethodChangesComplexity and limitation

Flash-style kernels target I/O and activation memory; sparse patterns target the number of score interactions; MQA/GQA target cached K/V heads during decoding. They solve different constraints and can be combined.

KV caching — memory grows linearly with served tokens

During autoregressive decoding, old token representations do not change. Cache each layer's K/V tensors so a new query attends to stored keys without re-projecting the prefix. Without a cache, repeatedly running full prefixes makes total attention work across an \(S\)-token generation grow cubically in the simple analysis; caching makes each step's attention linear in current prefix and total attention quadratic. The cache size is

$$\mathrm{bytes}_{KV}=2LBSn_{kv}d_{head}b,$$

where the leading 2 counts keys and values and \(b\) is bytes per stored element.

8.2 Generative architectures — three ways to invent data

Purpose — learn a distribution, not one best prediction

A generative model represents how plausible data are distributed so it can sample, reconstruct, edit, score, or complete observations. The families below move uncertainty differently: a VAE samples an explicit latent, a GAN learns through a discriminator, diffusion reverses gradual corruption, and an autoregressive model factors a joint distribution into conditional steps. Sample quality alone is insufficient—coverage, likelihood or calibration, controllability, speed, memorization, and evaluation all matter.

FamilyTraining signalSampling pathCharacteristic trade-off
VAEreconstruction likelihood plus latent-prior regularizationone latent draw then decoderfast sampling and useful latents; likelihood choice and posterior collapse can limit fidelity
GANadversarial real/fake gameone noise draw then generatorfast, sharp samples; unstable game and incomplete mode coverage
diffusiondenoise known corruptions or predict score/noise/velocityiterative denoisingflexible high-quality generation; many sequential network evaluations
autoregressiveconditional log-likelihood under an orderingone element/token after anothertractable factorized likelihood; serial sampling and order sensitivity

Variational autoencoders — optimize reconstruction and a usable latent prior

A VAE encoder produces an approximate posterior \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\), while the decoder defines \(p_\theta(x\mid z)\). For prior \(p(z)=\mathcal N(0,I)\), maximize the evidence lower bound \(\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-\beta D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p(z))\), or minimize reconstruction loss plus \(\beta KL\). For a diagonal Gaussian with means \(\mu_j\) and log variances \(\ell_j=\log\sigma_j^2\), \(KL=\frac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+e^{\ell_j}-\ell_j-1)\).

The reparameterization \(z=\mu+\exp(\!\tfrac12\ell)\odot\epsilon\), \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), moves randomness into an input so gradients can flow through \(\mu\) and \(\ell\). It does not remove Monte Carlo noise; it creates a lower-variance pathwise estimator under suitable distributions.

Guided calculation — diagonal-Gaussian VAE loss
Enter comma-separated means and log variances with equal length.
KL 1.307 reconstruction + \(\beta KL\) 3.053

Defaults give \(KL=\tfrac12[(1+1-0-1)+(0+4-\log4-1)]\approx1.307\), then \(2.4+0.5(1.307)\approx3.053\). The units of reconstruction loss depend on the decoder likelihood.

TRAIN_VAE(batch):
  mu, log_variance = ENCODER(batch)
  epsilon = SAMPLE_STANDARD_NORMAL(shape(mu))
  latent = mu + EXP(0.5 * log_variance) * epsilon
  reconstruction_distribution = DECODER(latent)
  reconstruction_loss = NEGATIVE_LOG_LIKELIHOOD(batch, reconstruction_distribution)
  kl = 0.5 * SUM(mu^2 + EXP(log_variance) - log_variance - 1)
  update encoder and decoder using reconstruction_loss + beta * kl
VAE failure modes

An overly expressive decoder can ignore \(z\), producing posterior collapse and near-zero KL. Excessive KL weight can erase information; too little can make prior samples poor. Blurry means often reflect the chosen output likelihood and averaging of uncertain pixels, not a universal property of every VAE. Inspect reconstruction, prior samples, KL per dimension, and downstream latent usefulness.

Generative adversarial networks — a learned critic creates the training signal

The original game is \(\min_G\max_D\;\mathbb E_{x\sim p_{data}}\log D(x)+\mathbb E_{z\sim p(z)}\log(1-D(G(z)))\). Under idealized capacity and optimization, its equilibrium matches the data distribution. In practice, practitioners often use the non-saturating generator loss \(-\log D(G(z))\) because it provides stronger early gradients. Alternating updates make a coupled, non-stationary optimization problem rather than ordinary descent on one fixed loss.

Worked adversarial losses

If \(D(x)=0.8\) for a real sample and \(D(G(z))=0.25\) for a fake, the discriminator's negative log objective is \(-[\log0.8+\log0.75]\approx0.511\). The non-saturating generator loss is \(-\log0.25\approx1.386\). These numbers are optimization signals, not standalone sample-quality scores.

TRAIN_GAN(real_batch):
  repeat discriminator_steps:
      noise = SAMPLE_PRIOR()
      fake = STOP_GRADIENT(GENERATOR(noise))
      update discriminator to score real high and fake low
  noise = SAMPLE_PRIOR()
  fresh_fake = GENERATOR(noise)
  update generator using non_saturating_loss(DISCRIMINATOR(fresh_fake))
  monitor quality and coverage on held-out data, not losses alone

Mode collapse occurs when many latent inputs map to too few output modes. A weak or overconfident discriminator, imbalanced update rates, limited data, and game dynamics can all contribute. Regularization, alternative divergences, gradient penalties, spectral normalization, augmentation, and architecture changes can help, but none guarantees convergence. Compare sample precision and recall or task-specific coverage rather than trusting one visual grid or FID alone.

Diffusion forward process — sample any noise level directly

Choose small noise increments \(\beta_t\), define \(\alpha_t=1-\beta_t\) and \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^t\alpha_s\). The Markov forward process has a closed-form marginal: \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon\), \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\). Training can therefore sample a random \(t\) and construct \(x_t\) in one operation rather than simulating every earlier step. A common simplified objective is \(\mathbb E\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,c)\|^2\), possibly with conditioning \(c\), weighting, or alternative \(x_0\)/velocity targets.

Worked forward sample

For scalar \(x_0=2\), sampled \(\epsilon=-1\), and \(\bar\alpha_t=0.81\), \(x_t=0.9(2)+\sqrt{0.19}(-1)\approx1.364\). Signal-to-noise ratio is \(\bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)=0.81/0.19\approx4.263\).

Reverse diffusion, DDPM, and DDIM — same training network, different paths

A learned denoiser parameterizes reverse transitions from noise toward data. DDPM-style ancestral sampling is stochastic: each step uses a predicted mean plus prescribed noise except at the final step. DDIM constructs a non-Markovian family compatible with the same common training objective; with its stochasticity parameter set to zero, the path is deterministic for a fixed initial noise and can use a shorter subset of time steps. Fewer evaluations reduce latency but may change fidelity, diversity, and numerical error. Modern solvers extend this trade-off; “DDPM means 1,000 steps” and “DDIM means exactly 50” are historical defaults, not definitions.

Physics bridge — diffusion and the score field

The forward process progressively relaxes data toward a Gaussian, analogous to a variance-preserving stochastic diffusion. The reverse-time dynamics depend on \(\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\). Under the common noise parameterization, predicted noise is proportional to the negative score at that noise level. The analogy guides derivation, but finite schedules and neural approximation determine the model.

Diffusion versus autoregressive generation

Autoregressive generation factorizes an ordered object as \(p(x)=\prod_i p(x_i\mid x_{<i})\), yielding a normalized conditional likelihood and natural streaming for discrete sequences. Training can evaluate positions in parallel under teacher forcing, but sampling follows the order serially. Diffusion predicts all positions at a noise level in parallel, but denoising steps are sequential. Latent diffusion moves the iterative process into a compressed space; discrete diffusion and autoregressive image models show that modality is not destiny.

8.3 Graphs and multimodality

Purpose — respect graph structure and node-label symmetry

A graph \(G=(V,E)\) represents entities and relations without a canonical node order. Relabeling nodes should relabel node outputs consistently (permutation equivariance), while a graph-level prediction should remain unchanged (permutation invariance). A message-passing GNN achieves this by sharing functions and using order-invariant neighbor aggregation:

$$m_v^{(k)}=\bigoplus_{u\in N(v)}\psi_k(h_v^{(k)},h_u^{(k)},e_{uv}),\qquad h_v^{(k+1)}=\phi_k(h_v^{(k)},m_v^{(k)}).$$

After \(k\) rounds, information can reach a node along paths of at most \(k\) edges, subject to direction and the chosen neighborhood. Graph-level tasks add an invariant readout over node states.

Message passing by hand — aggregate first, then update

Worked scalar update

A center node has \(h_v=2\) and neighbor values \([1,3,5]\). Mean aggregation gives \(m_v=3\). With \(h'_v=\mathrm{ReLU}(0.5h_v+0.25m_v)\), the result is \(\mathrm{ReLU}(1+0.75)=1.75\). Sum aggregation would give 9 and a different scale; degree normalization is therefore part of the model, not a cosmetic choice.

MESSAGE_PASS(node_states, edges):
  initialize one empty message collection per destination node
  for each directed edge (source, destination):
      append MESSAGE(node_states[source], node_states[destination], edge_features)
  for each node:
      aggregate messages with an order-invariant operation
      update node from its previous state and aggregate
  return updated node states

CLIP-style contrastive alignment — matched pairs compete in both directions

An image encoder and text encoder map paired items into a shared width. After L2 normalization, cosine similarities form \(S_{ij}=z_i^\top t_j\). Divide by temperature \(\tau\), then apply cross-entropy so image \(i\) selects its paired text and text \(i\) selects its paired image. The symmetric loss is the average of image-to-text and text-to-image losses. In-batch candidates make training efficient, but semantically valid unpaired items can act as false negatives.

Guided calculation — 2 × 2 symmetric contrastive loss
Diagonal entries are the matched pairs. Values represent cosine similarities.
image→text correct probabilities 0.9975, 0.9975 text→image correct probabilities 0.9991, 0.9933 symmetric loss 0.0031

Lower temperature sharpens the distribution and gradients; it does not add semantic evidence. With defaults, row logit gaps are 6 and column gaps are 7 and 5, giving mean cross-entropy about 0.0031.

TRAIN_CONTRASTIVE(image_batch, text_batch):
  images = NORMALIZE(IMAGE_ENCODER(image_batch))
  texts = NORMALIZE(TEXT_ENCODER(text_batch))
  logits = MATMUL(images, TRANSPOSE(texts)) / temperature
  labels = [0, 1, ..., batch_size - 1]
  loss_i2t = CROSS_ENTROPY(logits, labels)
  loss_t2i = CROSS_ENTROPY(TRANSPOSE(logits), labels)
  update both encoders and temperature using 0.5 * (loss_i2t + loss_t2i)

Multimodal use and failure checklist

Shared embeddings enable cross-modal retrieval and zero-shot classification by comparing an image with label prompts. Performance depends on the prompt, candidate labels, pretraining distribution, language, image style, and cultural context. Similarity is association, not grounded truth or a causal explanation. Duplicated pairs can leak evaluation data; web captions can encode stereotypes; and a single caption rarely exhausts the valid meanings of an image.

Self-check

1. Under independent zero-mean, unit-variance query/key components, why scale a dot product by \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\)?

b. Variances of the \(d_k\) product terms add. Scaling controls softmax saturation; learned correlations mean it is an approximate variance argument, not an entropy guarantee.

2. Cross-attention has \(B=2\), \(h=8\), 4 query positions, 6 key positions, and \(d_k=64\). Which shape/count is correct?

c. Scores have shape [B, h, nq, nk], so \(2\times8\times4\times6=384\).

3. Which transformer statement is technically defensible?

d. Norm placement affects optimization but admits successful variants. Being defined at a longer index is not the same as learned long-context competence.

4. Which efficiency comparison is correct?

a. These mechanisms target different bottlenecks. A cached new query still attends across its stored prefix, so per-step attention grows with context.

5. A diagonal VAE has \(\mu=[1,0]\), log variance \([0,\log4]\), reconstruction loss 2.4, and \(\beta=0.5\). Approximately what are KL and total loss?

c. \(KL=\frac12\sum(\mu^2+e^\ell-\ell-1)\approx1.307\), then \(2.4+0.5(1.307)\approx3.053\).

6. A GAN produces sharp samples but repeats a small set of nearly identical outputs. What diagnosis and evaluation response fit?

b. GAN losses and sharp examples can coexist with poor distribution coverage. Posterior collapse is the analogous latent-usage failure associated with VAEs.

7. For \(x_0=2\), \(\epsilon=-1\), and \(\bar\alpha_t=0.81\), what is the forward diffusion sample?

d. \(x_t=\sqrt{0.81}(2)+\sqrt{0.19}(-1)=1.8-0.436\approx1.364\).

8. Which comparison of DDPM/DDIM and autoregressive generation is correct?

c. Step counts are settings, not definitions. Both families have sequential axes—denoising steps versus emitted elements—with different parallel work inside each step.

9. A scalar GNN node has self value 2, neighbors [1,3,5], mean aggregation, self weight 0.5, message weight 0.25, zero bias, and ReLU. Its new value is:

a. Neighbor mean is 3, so \(\mathrm{ReLU}(0.5(2)+0.25(3))=1.75\). Sum aggregation would change the scale.

10. What does a CLIP-style symmetric contrastive loss do?

b. The loss is bidirectional cross-entropy over pair similarities. In-batch negatives can include semantically valid alternatives, so data construction remains important.
