Reinforcement Learning

Supervised learning copies answers; reinforcement learning discovers them by acting and being scored. It's the mathematics of sequential decisions under uncertainty — and, since RLHF, the machinery that turns raw language models into aligned assistants. The exam wants you fluent in the MDP formalism, the Bellman equations, and the practical differences among the algorithm families.

Learning outcomes
  • Formulate problems as MDPs (states, actions, rewards, transitions, discount).
  • Derive the Bellman equations; explain value vs policy iteration.
  • Compare Q-learning, SARSA, DQN; describe REINFORCE, A2C, PPO and the baseline's variance-reduction role.
  • Explain exploration–exploitation (ε-greedy, UCB, Thompson sampling).
  • Describe RLHF (reward modeling, the PPO loop) and compare with DPO.

11.1 The MDP formalism and the Bellman equations

A Markov Decision Process is \((S, A, P, R, \gamma)\): states, actions, transition probabilities \(P(s'\mid s,a)\), rewards \(R(s,a)\), and a discount \(\gamma \in [0,1)\) that makes the infinite future summable and encodes impatience. The Markov property — the future depends only on the current state — is what makes the math tractable. A policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\) is a behavior; its value is expected discounted return: \(V^\pi(s) = E\left[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t \mid s_0 = s\right]\).

The Bellman equations come from splitting that sum after one step. For the optimal value function: $$V^*(s) = \max_a \Big[ R(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\, V^*(s') \Big],$$ and the action-value version \(Q^*(s,a)\) (value of doing \(a\) now, then acting optimally) with \(V^*(s) = \max_a Q^*(s,a)\). These are self-consistency conditions — the value of a state is today's reward plus the discounted value of where you land.

⚛ Physics bridge

The Bellman equation is the discrete Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation: \(V\) plays the role of an action functional / potential, and the greedy policy "follow \(\arg\max\)" is steepest descent on it. Value iteration is a fixed-point relaxation — the Bellman operator is a γ-contraction (Banach fixed point theorem guarantees convergence), exactly like relaxing a lattice field to equilibrium. And watch the widget: value literally diffuses backward from the reward like heat from a source, attenuated by γ per hop.

With a known model, value iteration applies the Bellman update everywhere until convergence; policy iteration alternates full policy evaluation with greedy improvement — fewer, costlier sweeps. Both are dynamic programming: exact, and doomed by the curse of dimensionality beyond small state spaces — hence learning.

11.2 Model-free control — learning Q from experience

Q-learning updates toward the Bellman target using samples: $$Q(s,a) \mathrel{+}= \alpha \big[\, r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a) \,\big],$$ the bracket being the temporal-difference (TD) error. The \(\max\) makes it off-policy: it learns the value of acting greedily even while behaving exploratorily. SARSA replaces \(\max_{a'} Q(s',a')\) with \(Q(s',a')\) for the action actually taken — on-policy, so it learns the value of its real (ε-noisy) behavior: near a cliff, SARSA learns the safe path, Q-learning learns the optimal-but-risky one. DQN scales Q-learning with a neural network over raw states (Atari pixels), stabilized by two tricks worth naming: experience replay (train on shuffled past transitions — restoring the i.i.d.-ish data SGD wants) and a target network (a frozen copy providing the bootstrap target so the regression isn't chasing itself).

11.3 Policy gradients — optimize the behavior directly

Value methods struggle with continuous actions and inherently stochastic optima. Policy-gradient methods parameterize \(\pi_\theta\) directly and ascend expected return. The policy-gradient theorem / REINFORCE: $$\nabla_\theta J = E\big[\, G_t \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \,\big]$$ — increase log-probability of actions in proportion to the return that followed. Unbiased, and ferociously noisy: \(G_t\) mixes the credit of one action with everything else that happened. Subtracting a baseline \(b(s)\) — any function of state, typically a learned \(V(s)\) — leaves the gradient unbiased (\(E[b\,\nabla\log\pi] = 0\), since probabilities sum to 1) but slashes variance: what remains is the advantage \(A = G - V(s)\), "how much better than expected." That's A2C: an actor (policy) plus a critic (baseline).

PPO adds the industrial safety rail. Policy gradients are only valid near the current policy; a large step can collapse performance irrecoverably (the data you collect next comes from the new, broken policy). PPO clips the update: with ratio \(\rho = \pi_{new}(a|s)/\pi_{old}(a|s)\), it optimizes \(\min(\rho A, \; \mathrm{clip}(\rho, 1\pm\epsilon) A)\) — no incentive to move the ratio beyond ±ε per update. Simple, robust, and the default RL algorithm of the LLM era.

11.4 Exploration — the bandit laboratory

The tension: exploit the best-known option or explore alternatives that might be better. The clean testbed is the multi-armed bandit (one state, K unknown reward rates). ε-greedy: random arm with probability ε — simple, never stops wasting ε of its pulls. UCB: pick \(\arg\max_a \hat\mu_a + c\sqrt{\ln t / n_a}\) — "optimism in the face of uncertainty," exploring exactly what is under-sampled, with logarithmic regret. Thompson sampling: keep a posterior per arm (Beta for Bernoulli — Topic 2), sample one draw from each, act on the sample — exploration emerges from posterior uncertainty and shuts itself off as beliefs sharpen.

ε-greedy's regret grows linearly forever (it never stops exploring at rate ε); UCB and Thompson bend logarithmic. Set ε = 0 and re-run a few times: sometimes fine, sometimes locked onto a bad arm permanently — the same failure you saw in the gridworld. Production systems (ad ranking, A/B platforms, RAG router selection) are bandits wearing business clothes.

11.5 RLHF — reinforcement learning meets language models

A pretrained LLM is a policy: state = the context so far, action = next token, episode = the response. But there's no natural reward. RLHF manufactures one:

  1. Collect preferences: humans compare pairs of model responses (A ≻ B) — far easier and more reliable than absolute scoring.
  2. Train a reward model — usually a copy of the LLM with a scalar head — via the Bradley–Terry likelihood \(P(A \succ B) = \sigma(r(A) - r(B))\): a learned stand-in for human judgment.
  3. Optimize the policy with PPO against the reward model, with a KL penalty to the original model: \(\max_\pi E[r(x, y)] - \beta\, D_{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{ref})\). The KL term is load-bearing — it keeps the policy from wandering into regions where the reward model is wrong and exploitable (reward hacking — the subject of Topics 17 and 19).

DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) collapses stages 2–3: the KL-constrained RLHF objective has a closed-form optimal policy, and substituting it back turns preference learning into a simple classification-style loss on the policy itself — no reward model, no sampling loop, no PPO machinery. In exchange, you lose the ability to sample fresh responses during optimization (DPO is offline, bound to its preference dataset) and the explicit reward model that RLHF can reuse for filtering and monitoring. Level III (Topic 17) makes choosing between them a design decision; here, know the mechanics cold.

Jargon decoder
return / reward
One step's score vs the discounted sum over the future — the thing actually maximized.
on-policy / off-policy
Learning about the policy you're executing (SARSA, PPO) vs about a different (greedy) one (Q-learning, DQN).
model-based / model-free
Uses known/learned dynamics P(s′|s,a) (value iteration, MuZero) vs learns from transitions alone.
TD error
r + γV(s′) − V(s): the surprise; the universal learning signal (and dopamine's best computational model).
advantage
Q(s,a) − V(s): how much better an action is than the policy's average — the variance-reduced credit signal.
regret
Cumulative shortfall vs always playing the optimal action; the bandit performance metric.
reward model (RM)
Learned scorer of responses trained from human pairwise preferences.
Bradley–Terry
The pairwise-comparison probability model σ(r_A − r_B) behind RM training (and chess Elo).

Self-check

1. In cliff-walking with ε-greedy behavior, SARSA learns a path far from the cliff while Q-learning hugs the edge. Why?

c. The canonical on-/off-policy exam question (Sutton & Barto Example 6.6). Q-learning is right about the greedy policy but the greedy policy isn't what's being executed during learning.

2. Subtracting a state-value baseline from returns in REINFORCE:

b. ∑_a π(a|s) = 1 ⇒ ∑_a ∇π = 0, so any state-only baseline integrates to zero against the score function. This unbiased-variance-reduction argument is a standard derivation request.

3. Remove the KL penalty from RLHF's objective and train PPO hard against the reward model. Expected outcome:

d. The RM is only accurate near the data it was trained on. Unconstrained optimization against a learned proxy is Goodhart's law in action — the mechanism behind reward hacking (Topics 17/19).

4. Your team must choose between RLHF and DPO for preference-tuning with a fixed dataset of 80k comparisons, no budget for online sampling. Recommend:

a. DPO's sweet spot is exactly this: fixed offline preferences, limited infra. RLHF earns its complexity when you can sample fresh responses and iterate the RM (Topic 17 develops the full decision).
