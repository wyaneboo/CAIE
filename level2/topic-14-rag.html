Retrieval, RAG & Vector Systems

An LLM's weights are a photograph of its training data — frozen, undated, and unable to cite a source. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) bolts a library onto the model: find the relevant documents at question time, paste them into the context, and let the model read rather than recall. It is currently the most-deployed LLM architecture in industry, and its quality is decided almost entirely by the retrieval half — which is classical information retrieval plus vector geometry, all of it measurable.

Learning outcomes
  • Compare sparse (TF-IDF, BM25) and dense retrieval; explain hybrid search and RRF.
  • Describe embedding models and ANN indexes: HNSW, IVF, product quantization.
  • Analyze chunking strategies and their effect on retrieval quality.
  • Explain re-ranking and the bi-encoder vs cross-encoder tradeoff.
  • Diagnose retrieval drift and context-relevance failures.
  • Describe query expansion, multi-hop, and adaptive/agentic RAG.
  • Evaluate a RAG system: faithfulness, context precision/recall, answer relevance.

14.1 Two ways to find a document — words vs meaning

Sparse retrieval matches words. Documents become vectors with one dimension per vocabulary term (almost all zeros — hence sparse), searched via an inverted index: term → list of documents containing it, the data structure behind every search engine since the 1970s. Scoring is TF-IDF refined into BM25: $$\text{score}(q,d)=\sum_{t\in q}\underbrace{\ln\!\Big(1+\tfrac{N-n_t+0.5}{n_t+0.5}\Big)}_{\text{IDF: rare terms count}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{f_{t,d}\,(k_1+1)}{f_{t,d}+k_1\big(1-b+b\,\tfrac{|d|}{\overline{|d|}}\big)}}_{\text{saturating TF, length-normalized}}$$ Two design ideas worth naming: rare terms carry more information (IDF — Shannon again, Topic 2), and term frequency saturates (the tenth occurrence of "password" adds almost nothing — \(k_1\) sets the saturation scale, \(b\) penalizes long documents).

Dense retrieval matches meaning. An embedding model (a transformer trained contrastively — CLIP's trick from Topic 8, applied to query/passage pairs) maps text to a single vector of ~768–3072 dimensions; relevance is cosine similarity. Synonyms land close ("turn off alerts" ≈ "disable notifications") with zero word overlap. The failure mode is the mirror image: exact strings — product IDs, error codes, names, version numbers — blur into the semantic soup, while BM25 nails them via a huge IDF.

Hence hybrid search, the production default: run both, merge. Score fusion (weighted sum of normalized scores) or reciprocal rank fusion (RRF): \(\sum_r 1/(60 + \text{rank}_r)\) — rank-based, so no score-calibration headaches. Try to break each retriever below.

14.2 Searching a billion vectors — ANN indexes

Exact nearest-neighbor search is a linear scan: \(O(N \cdot d)\) — 1B vectors × 1536 dims × 4 bytes = 6 TB read per query. Nobody does that. Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) indexes trade a little recall for orders of magnitude in speed:

  • IVF (inverted file): k-means the vectors into \(k\) cells offline; at query time, find the nprobe nearest centroids and scan only those cells. Recall knob = nprobe: more cells scanned, better recall, more work. Fails when the true neighbor sits just across a cell boundary — the edge effect you'll see below.
  • HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world): a layered proximity graph. Greedy-walk toward the query on a sparse top layer (long hops), drop a layer, refine — like a skip list in vector space. \(O(\log N)\)-ish hops, excellent recall, the default for in-memory indexes; costs memory (the graph) and slow inserts.
  • Product quantization (PQ): compression, composable with both. Split each vector into \(m\) sub-vectors; k-means each sub-space into 256 codewords; store one byte per sub-vector. A 1536-d float vector (6 KB) becomes, say, 96 bytes — 64× smaller — and distances are computed against the codebooks without decompressing. Recall drops; a re-check of top candidates against full vectors ("refine") recovers most of it.
⚛ Physics bridge

Coarse-to-fine search is multigrid / renormalization thinking: solve on the coarse lattice (IVF centroids, HNSW's top layer), then refine locally. And embedding spaces live in the regime of concentration of measure: in 1000 dimensions, random vectors are almost surely almost orthogonal, and distances concentrate tightly around their mean — the same concentration that makes statistical mechanics work. Similarity search is only meaningful because trained embeddings occupy a low-dimensional manifold inside that space; on truly random data, "nearest" neighbor would barely beat noise.

14.3 The RAG pipeline — chunking, retrieval, re-ranking

The standard pipeline: split documents into chunks → embed chunks → index → (query) retrieve top-k → re-rank → stuff the winners into the prompt. Two of those steps hide most of the quality variance:

Chunking. Embeddings summarize a chunk into one vector, so chunk size is a microscope setting. Too small: precise matches, but facts get severed from the context that makes them answerable (and multi-sentence answers arrive amputated). Too large: recall of "somewhere in here" rises, but the vector dilutes (mean of many topics), ranking margins shrink, and you ship mostly-irrelevant tokens to the model — paying per token for noise that also distracts it. Practical defaults: 200–500 tokens, split on structure (headings, paragraphs) rather than fixed character counts, with 10–20% overlap so boundary-straddling facts survive. Semantic chunking (split where embedding similarity between consecutive sentences dips) is the fancier version of the same idea.

Re-ranking. The retriever is a bi-encoder: query and document embedded separately, similarity = one dot product. That independence is what makes indexing possible (documents embedded offline), and it's also the ceiling — the model never sees query and document together. A cross-encoder feeds the concatenated pair through a transformer so attention can compare them token by token: far more accurate, and O(one forward pass per candidate) — unusable for the whole corpus. The standard architecture is a funnel: bi-encoder retrieves 100 candidates from millions; cross-encoder re-ranks those 100; the top 5 go in the prompt. Cheap-and-wide, then expensive-and-narrow.

14.4 When RAG fails — and how to measure it

RAG failures decompose cleanly because the pipeline does: was the right context retrieved (retrieval failure), and did the model use it honestly (generation failure). The evaluation triad, one metric per joint:

  • Context recall / precision: did the retrieved chunks contain the needed evidence, and how much of what was retrieved was actually relevant? (The two curves in the widget above.) Retrieval-side; measured against a golden set of question → evidence pairs.
  • Faithfulness (groundedness): is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved context? Unfaithful answers with perfect retrieval mean the model ignored its reading and recalled from weights — the failure RAG exists to prevent. Measured claim-by-claim, usually by an LLM judge (Topic 9's evaluation caveats apply).
  • Answer relevance: does the answer actually address the question? Faithful non-answers ("the document discusses passwords") score high on groundedness and zero here.

Chronic production issues to name in vignettes: retrieval drift — the corpus grows and shifts while the embedding model (or its version!) stays frozen; new jargon embeds poorly, and mixing vectors from two embedder versions in one index silently corrupts all similarities (vectors from different models are not comparable — re-embed everything on upgrade). Lost in the middle — models attend best to the start and end of a long context, so evidence buried mid-prompt underperforms; order retrieved chunks by relevance, best first or last. And stale-index skew — documents updated in the source but not re-embedded, RAG's version of Topic 13's train/serve skew.

14.5 Advanced patterns — expansion, multi-hop, agentic RAG

  • Query expansion / rewriting: user queries are terse and vocabulary-mismatched; have an LLM rewrite or expand them before retrieval. HyDE flips it: generate a hypothetical answer, embed that, and search with it — answers live nearer to documents than questions do in embedding space.
  • Multi-hop retrieval: "Which university did the CEO of the company that makes Ozempic attend?" needs two lookups, the second parameterized by the first. Decompose, retrieve, chain — no single query lands it.
  • Adaptive / agentic RAG: let the model decide — whether to retrieve at all (many questions don't need it), reformulate and re-retrieve when results look weak, or self-critique grounding before answering (Self-RAG). This is retrieval becoming a tool in an agent loop — the bridge to Topic 18.
Jargon decoder
inverted index
Term → list of documents containing it; the sparse-search data structure (Lucene, Elasticsearch).
BM25
The standard lexical scorer: IDF × saturating, length-normalized term frequency. Still the baseline to beat.
bi-encoder / cross-encoder
Embed query and doc separately (indexable, fast) vs jointly attend to the pair (accurate, per-candidate cost). Funnel: first retrieves, second re-ranks.
RRF
Reciprocal rank fusion — merge ranked lists via Σ 1/(60+rank); calibration-free hybrid merging.
HNSW / IVF / PQ
Layered proximity graph / k-means cells with nprobe / codebook compression — the three ANN building blocks, often combined (e.g., IVF-PQ).
recall@k
Fraction of queries whose true nearest neighbor (or gold document) appears in the top k returned.
faithfulness
Every claim in the generated answer is supported by the retrieved context; the anti-hallucination metric.
HyDE
Hypothetical Document Embeddings — embed a generated guess-answer and search with it.
lost in the middle
LLMs use context edges better than the middle; chunk placement matters, not just retrieval.
golden set
Curated question → evidence → answer triples used to score a RAG pipeline offline before users do.

Self-check

1. Users searching your product docs for error codes ("E4021") get irrelevant results; conversational queries work fine. Your retriever is dense-only. Best fix:

b. The embedder likely tokenizes "E4021" into meaningless fragments with no semantic content — no dimension of the vector encodes it. Hybrid search exists precisely for the ID/code/name query class. (d) ships more noise; (a) and (c) don't address the mechanism.

2. Your IVF index (nprobe = 1) has recall@10 of 71%; the product needs 95%+ at the lowest latency cost. First move:

c. nprobe is the designed recall/latency dial (the widget's curve). (b) also works but requires a rebuild and coarsens the whole index; (a) pays maximum latency; (d) changes semantics, not index recall. Exam pattern: prefer the reversible knob before the rebuild.

3. Why not run the cross-encoder over the whole 10M-document corpus, since it ranks better than the bi-encoder?

a. The bi-encoder's separability is a computational property, not an accuracy choice: doc vectors are query-independent, hence indexable. Joint attention destroys that. Hence the funnel: ANN over everything, cross-encoder over 100.

4. Retrieval metrics are excellent (context recall 0.96), but users report confident wrong answers. The RAG-triad diagnosis:

d. Good retrieval + wrong answers = the generation joint. Fixes in order: prompt-level grounding ("answer only from the context, cite chunk IDs"), citation verification, and if needed a groundedness checker gate before responding. This decomposition-by-pipeline-stage is the whole point of the triad.
