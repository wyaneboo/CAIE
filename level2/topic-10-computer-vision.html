Computer Vision

Vision was deep learning's first conquest, and its history is a compressed course in architecture design: how to go deep without dying (ResNet), how to scale sanely (EfficientNet), how to replace convolutions with attention (ViT), and how the same backbone becomes a detector, a segmenter, or an image generator with the right head bolted on.

Learning outcomes
  • Describe ResNet skip connections and EfficientNet compound scaling.
  • Explain Vision Transformers and patch embeddings.
  • Describe detection (YOLO, Faster R-CNN) and segmentation (U-Net, Mask R-CNN).
  • Apply transfer learning/fine-tuning; describe augmentation and its regularizing effect.
  • Explain latent-diffusion generation and editing pipelines.

10.1 Modern CNN backbones

ResNet answered the paradox of 2015: adding layers made networks worse — not from overfitting but from optimization failure (deeper nets couldn't even fit the training set). The fix is one line: make each block compute a residual, \(h_{out} = h_{in} + F(h_{in})\). Now the identity is the default — a block only has to learn a correction, and gradients flow through the addition untouched (Topic 5's LSTM logic in spatial form). Result: 152-layer networks trained where 30 had failed, and skip connections became universal — every transformer block carries them.

EfficientNet systematized "make it bigger": instead of scaling depth or width or input resolution ad hoc, scale all three by fixed ratios from a budget-multiplier — compound scaling. Its deeper lesson survived into the LLM era: the question is never "bigger?" but "bigger in what proportions?" — which is exactly the Chinchilla question of Topic 16, asked three years early.

10.2 Vision Transformers

ViT makes the bet that with enough data, learned attention beats built-in convolutional bias: cut the image into fixed patches (16×16), flatten each into a vector, project linearly — these are your "tokens" — add positional embeddings and run a standard transformer encoder. A special [CLS] token's final state (or a pooled mean) is the image representation. With small data, CNNs win (their translation-equivariance is free knowledge); past tens of millions of images, ViT's flexibility wins. Patch size is the resolution/cost dial: sequence length = (H/P)·(W/P), and attention is quadratic in it.

10.3 Detection and segmentation — one backbone, different heads

Detection = "what and where": output boxes + classes. Faster R-CNN is the accurate two-stage design: a Region Proposal Network suggests candidate boxes over backbone features, then a second head classifies and refines each — two passes, high accuracy, ~real-time only with effort. YOLO is the fast one-stage design: divide the image into a grid; each cell directly regresses boxes, objectness, and class in a single forward pass — the default for video and edge deployment. Both produce overlapping candidates and rely on non-maximum suppression: keep the highest-scoring box, delete neighbors whose IoU (intersection-over-union) with it exceeds a threshold, repeat. Detection metrics (mAP) hinge on IoU thresholds — build the intuition below.

Interactive — IoU, the metric of overlap
Drag the predicted box (blue) over the ground truth (gray). IoU = intersection area / union area. Detection scoring counts a prediction correct only above a threshold (commonly 0.5).

Segmentation = per-pixel labels. U-Net is the canonical architecture: a contracting encoder (context) and an expanding decoder (localization) with skip connections copying early high-resolution features across to the decoder at matching scales — without them, precise boundaries are unrecoverable after downsampling. Born in medical imaging; now also the standard denoising backbone inside diffusion models. Mask R-CNN unifies the threads: Faster R-CNN plus a small mask head per detected object — instance segmentation (each object separately) versus U-Net-style semantic segmentation (each class, all instances merged).

10.4 Transfer learning and augmentation

Transfer learning is the default workflow: take a backbone pretrained on millions of images (ImageNet, or CLIP-style web pairs — Topic 8), and adapt: (1) freeze it and train only a new head — right when data is scarce; (2) fine-tune the last blocks with a small learning rate — more data; (3) fine-tune everything — lots of data, still with reduced LR to avoid destroying pretrained features (catastrophic forgetting). Early layers hold universal edges/textures; later layers hold task-specific semantics — freeze from the bottom up.

Augmentation multiplies your dataset with label-preserving transforms: flips, crops, rotations, color jitter, plus the modern composites — MixUp (blend two images and their labels), CutMix (paste a patch of one image into another), RandAugment (random policy). The regularization view: you are declaring invariances — "a cat shifted left is still a cat" — thereby shrinking the hypothesis space exactly like a physics symmetry constraint. The caveat: augmentations must respect the label (flipping a chest X-ray horizontally moves the heart to the wrong side; rotating a "6" makes a "9").

10.5 Generation: latent diffusion pipelines

Topic 8 gave you diffusion; production image generation adds one crucial trick. Pixel-space diffusion at 1024² is brutally expensive, so latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion) runs the whole process in a compressed space: a VAE encoder squeezes the image ~48× (512×512×3 → 64×64×4 latents); diffusion happens there with a U-Net (or, in newer systems, a transformer — DiT) conditioned on text embeddings via cross-attention; the VAE decoder renders the final pixels. Editing falls out of the same machinery: img2img noises a real image partway and denoises with a new prompt (the t-slider from Topic 8's widget is the "strength" knob); inpainting masks a region and denoises only there; ControlNet bolts on spatial conditioning (edges, pose, depth) for layout control.

Interactive — receptive field growth through a CNN
Stack 3×3 conv layers (with an optional stride-2 layer) and see which input pixels influence one output unit.

Receptive field grows +2 pixels per 3×3 layer — but a stride-2 layer doubles the growth rate of everything after it. That's why CNNs downsample: it's the cheap route to seeing the whole image. (Attention gets global receptive field in one layer — and pays quadratically for it.)
