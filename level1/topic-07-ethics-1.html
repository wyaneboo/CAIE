Professional Ethics & Responsible AI I

Ethics is engineering under consequences: deciding whose evidence counts, which errors matter, what data are justified, and when uncertainty makes release irresponsible. The mathematical trade-offs are real, but mathematics does not choose whose risk to prioritize. Professional practice makes those value choices visible, reviewable, and accountable.

Learning outcomes
  • Describe the CAIE Code of Conduct and Standards of Professional Practice.
  • Define algorithmic bias and the major fairness criteria (demographic parity, equalized odds) — and explain why they are mutually incompatible.
  • Describe privacy principles (consent, minimization, purpose limitation) and major regulations (GDPR, EU AI Act risk tiers).
  • Explain informed consent and dual-use risk; describe the duty of competence.
Orientation — four layers of responsibility

Law sets enforceable minimum duties. Professional standards describe conduct expected of practitioners. Organizational policy assigns local controls and owners. Ethical reasoning examines harms and duties that compliance may not settle. A lawful system can still be harmful; a worthy aim does not waive the law.

Legal scope

The regulation summaries below are an educational overview, current to 18 August 2026—not legal advice. Jurisdiction, role, intended purpose, sector, and facts can change the result. For a real system, involve qualified privacy, legal, safety, security, and domain specialists.

7.1 The Code, compressed

Purpose — turn principles into repeatable professional decisions

The six items below are this course's synthesis of the CAIE Code, not a statutory licence or a substitute for an employer's formal code. Use them as tests: identify the duty, connect it to evidence, choose a proportionate action, and record who owns the residual risk. “Be ethical” is too vague to audit; “disclosed subgroup uncertainty and withheld launch approval pending review” is observable professional conduct.

Duties can point in different directions. Transparency may conflict with privacy or security; helping one group may shift errors to another; rapid access to a beneficial tool may increase unmeasured harm. A conflict is not permission to choose secretly—it is a reason to document the trade-off and seek the right authority.

Worked professional judgment — an unaudited launch

Scenario

A manager wants a hiring ranker released tomorrow. Overall validation is complete, but subgroup recall, accommodation effects, and appeal handling were not tested. The schedule is commercial; applicant opportunity is high-impact and errors may be difficult to reverse.

  1. State the gap precisely: “Performance for affected subgroups and the remedy process are unverified,” not “the model is biased” without evidence.
  2. Propose a proportionate remedy: complete the subgroup audit, test accessible alternatives, document decision support versus automation, and define an appeal route.
  3. Set a release condition: for this high-impact use, do not represent the system as validated until the blocking evidence and accountable approval exist.
  4. Escalate with evidence: preserve the concern, response, owner, and decision. Seek independent HR, legal, domain, and responsible-AI review.

Immediate resignation is not the automatic first move, and silent release is not acceptable. The escalation path depends on urgency, severity, law, organizational channels, and worker protections. An imminent serious threat can justify faster emergency escalation.

When to use TRACE

Use it before a material data or model change, when evidence conflicts with a release request, when affected people cannot easily contest an outcome, or whenever the team lacks authority or competence to accept the risk. Record only necessary evidence—an ethics log should not become a new store of sensitive personal data.

7.2 Algorithmic bias and the fairness impossibility

Purpose — locate bias across the whole decision system

In statistics, bias can mean systematic estimation error. In responsible-AI work, the concern is broader: a data-driven system can distribute errors, resources, surveillance, or opportunity unjustly. A metric gap is evidence to investigate, not by itself a complete causal or legal conclusion. The unit of analysis is the workflow—problem definition, data, label, model, threshold, interface, human decision, and feedback—not the model file alone.

Where bias enters — trace source, mechanism, and affected group

SourceMechanismExample diagnostic

“Fairness through unawareness” is not a test

Removing a protected attribute does not remove correlated proxies or historical structure. Auditing may require protected-attribute data under strict authorization and privacy controls; collecting or using those attributes can itself be legally sensitive. Obtain appropriate review rather than assuming either collection or deletion is universally required.

From confusion counts to group rates

For group \(a\), start with TP, FP, FN, and TN. Prevalence is \(\pi_a=(TP_a+FN_a)/N_a\); selection rate is \(SR_a=(TP_a+FP_a)/N_a\); true-positive rate is \(TPR_a=TP_a/(TP_a+FN_a)\); false-positive rate is \(FPR_a=FP_a/(FP_a+TN_a)\); and positive predictive value is \(PPV_a=TP_a/(TP_a+FP_a)\). A zero denominator makes a rate undefined, not zero.

Point estimates can be unstable for small groups. Report denominators and an appropriate uncertainty interval; avoid ranking tiny gaps as precise moral facts.

Demographic parity — compare selection rates

Demographic parity asks for \(P(\hat Y=1\mid A=a)\) to be equal across groups. It focuses on who receives the positive decision without conditioning on the observed outcome. A difference can be reported as \(SR_A-SR_B\), while a ratio such as \(SR_B/SR_A\) answers a different question and becomes unstable when the reference rate is near zero.

When it can illuminate—and when it can mislead

Selection parity can be relevant when the outcome is access to a scarce opportunity and labels reflect unequal prior access. It may be inappropriate as the only target when valid outcome needs differ, when intervention benefits everyone differently, or when the “positive” label is not a benefit. State the decision meaning before calling a larger selection rate better.

Equal opportunity and equalized odds — compare conditional errors

Equal opportunity asks for equal TPR: among people with observed \(Y=1\), each group is equally likely to receive \(\hat Y=1\). Equalized odds asks for both TPR and FPR to be equal, equivalently \(\hat Y\perp A\mid Y\) for a binary decision. These criteria treat the label as a meaningful reference. If the label is biased, selectively observed, or affected by earlier decisions, equalizing rates against it can reproduce the wrong target.

Which error matters depends on context. A high false-negative rate may deny a beneficial service; a high false-positive rate may expose people to an unnecessary investigation. The two harms can affect different people, so report both rather than hiding one behind “accuracy.”

Predictive parity is not score calibration

Predictive parity for a thresholded binary decision asks for equal PPV: \(P(Y=1\mid\hat Y=1,A=a)\). Calibration within groups is a score-level condition: for score \(S=s\), \(P(Y=1\mid S=s,A=a)=s\) (or the corresponding observed risk) across relevant score ranges. Equal PPV at one threshold does not establish calibration across scores, and a calibrated score can produce different thresholded PPV or error patterns when policies differ.

CriterionConditions onPrimary question

The incompatibility result — conditions matter

There is no theorem saying every fairness definition conflicts in every dataset. A precise binary result is: when group prevalences differ, a non-degenerate imperfect classifier generally cannot have equal TPR and FPR across groups and equal PPV. With equalized odds, \(PPV_a=\pi_aTPR/[\pi_aTPR+(1-\pi_a)FPR]\); if \(TPR\) and \(FPR\) are shared but \(\pi_a\) differs, PPV usually differs. Perfect prediction and other degenerate or constrained cases are exceptions. Related score results show tensions among calibration and balance conditions under unequal base rates.

Worked incompatibility example

Each group has 1,000 people. Group A prevalence is 60% and group B prevalence is 30%. Both have TPR 80% and FPR 20%. A therefore has TP 480, FN 120, FP 80, TN 320: selection 56% and PPV \(480/560=85.7\%\). B has TP 240, FN 60, FP 140, TN 560: selection 38% and PPV \(240/380=63.2\%\). Equalized odds holds, but selection parity and predictive parity do not.

Choose, audit, and govern a criterion

No metric can decide fairness without a purpose and harm model. Identify who bears false positives, false negatives, delay, or denial; examine whether \(Y\) is a defensible reference; consult affected stakeholders and domain experts; determine applicable law; then select metrics and remedy routes before tuning. Group-specific thresholds or treatment can alter trade-offs but may raise legal and policy constraints—do not prescribe them without qualified review.

Common failure modes

Average metrics can hide intersectional harm; too many tiny slices can create noisy conclusions; observed labels can reflect selective intervention; and “fixing” a metric can shift harm elsewhere. Use uncertainty, minimum support rules, qualitative evidence, and causal investigation. Fairness is a continuing governance process, not a one-time dashboard.

7.3 Privacy and regulation

Purpose — govern a data lifecycle, not just a database

Privacy concerns how information about people is collected, inferred, used, shared, retained, and contested—not merely whether a table is secret. Start with a declared purpose and follow data from collection through derived features, model artifacts, logs, backups, vendors, user interfaces, and deletion. Inferred attributes and embeddings can be personal data when they relate to an identifiable person even if the original name is absent.

GDPR — principles, lawful bases, rights, and qualified rules

GDPR territorial scope depends on establishment and processing context, including some offering of goods or services to, or monitoring of, people in the Union; “EU resident data” is an unsafe shortcut. Identify whether the organization is a controller deciding purposes and means, a processor acting on documented instructions, or another role. Special categories of data and criminal-conviction data have additional conditions.

Article 5 principleEngineering translation

Consent is one of six Article 6 lawful bases alongside contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, and legitimate interests; choosing a basis requires facts and legal analysis. When consent is used, Article 7 requires it to be demonstrable, clear, and withdrawable as easily as it was given; GDPR standards also require freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous agreement. Power imbalance can make consent unsuitable.

Data-subject rights include access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, and objection, but their scope and exceptions differ. Article 22 concerns decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects. Contract necessity, law, or explicit consent can create exceptions with safeguards; where applicable these include human intervention, expressing a view, and contesting the decision. This is more specific than a blanket “right to an explanation.” Certain serious infringements can reach the higher administrative-fine tier of €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, subject to the law's conditions and enforcement assessment.

Minimization and retention — reduce unnecessary exposure

Minimization asks whether each field, granularity, person, and use is necessary; storage limitation asks how long identifiability remains necessary. The simple exposure surface below multiplies records, retained fields, and months. It is useful for comparing two inventory designs, but it is not breach probability, legal compliance, or a risk score: one health field may matter more than many low-sensitivity fields.

For 100,000 records, 12 fields retained for 24 months produce 28.8 million illustrative record-field-months. EU AI Act — classify the role, purpose, and obligation as of August 2026

The EU AI Act is risk-based, but the familiar four “tiers” are teaching shorthand. Classification depends on the system's intended purpose, the actor's role (such as provider or deployer), sector, affected use, and exceptions. General-purpose AI (GPAI) obligations form a separate layer. As of 18 August 2026, the Act is generally applicable; prohibited-practice and AI-literacy provisions applied from 2 February 2025, GPAI rules from 2 August 2025, and Article 50 transparency duties from 2 August 2026. Following the July 2026 targeted amendments, Annex III high-risk rules are scheduled for 2 December 2027 and Annex I product-system rules for 2 August 2028. Verify current dates in the European Commission overview and the official regulation text.

7.4 Consent, dual use, and the duty of competence

Informed consent — design for a real choice, not a ceremonial click

Ethically meaningful consent requires understandable information, a specific choice, sufficient capacity or appropriate authorization, and freedom from manipulation or undue pressure. Under GDPR, valid consent has specific legal requirements and is only one possible lawful basis. Do not request consent when the organization would process regardless, or bundle an optional model-training use into a service that cannot realistically be refused.

Worked purpose-change analysis

Support tickets were collected to resolve customer problems. A team now wants to train a public language model on ticket text. Ask whether that new purpose and disclosure are compatible with the original basis and notice, whether tickets contain third-party or sensitive information, whether people had a genuine relevant choice, and whether a less intrusive dataset works. “The data are already in our database” answers none of those questions.

Dual use — build a threat model around access and scale

A voice-cloning system can restore speech and enable impersonation; a biological design model can support medicine and harmful synthesis. Calling a tool “general purpose” does not remove foreseeable misuse. Threat modelling asks: who might misuse which capability, through what access path, at what scale, with what severity and reversibility? It also asks what evidence shows a control works and what residual risk remains.

Guided triage — ordinal dual-use review
A structured prompt for escalation, not a probability model, legal test, or release authorization.
ordinal score 12 triage pause and escalate

The illustrative score is \(\max(4, severity+reach+ease+irreversibility-mitigation)\): at most it prioritizes review. Scores 4–6 suggest documented routine controls, 7–10 independent review, and 11 or more a pause and escalation. A severity rating of 4 forces escalation even if the sum is lower. Do not average away a catastrophic pathway, vulnerable population, legal prohibition, or unknown control failure.

Controls interact: logging can help abuse response but create a sensitive surveillance dataset; identity checks can deter abuse but exclude legitimate anonymous use. Test controls against realistic adversaries, document bypasses, and reassess after scale or capability changes. “Residual risk” is what remains after credited controls—not what the team prefers not to discuss.

Duty of competence — know the system's domain and your own

Competence is not knowing everything; it is recognizing what the decision requires, producing evidence within your expertise, and seeking qualified review for the rest. Model accuracy does not establish domain validity, security, legal compliance, human-factors safety, or operational reliability. A competent team monitors the assumptions that justified release and knows when a change invalidates them.

Physics bridge — domain of validity

Treat a model like an approximation with boundary conditions. Declare the population, environment, measurement process, time period, intervention, and acceptable error. Outside that domain, a numerical score may still appear while its evidential warrant has vanished.

Escalation — match the path to urgency, authority, and harm

  1. Verify and bound: preserve evidence, separate fact from inference, and avoid unnecessary personal data.
  2. Raise with the responsible owner: state the duty, affected people, uncertainty, and a practical remedy.
  3. Seek independent review: involve domain, privacy, legal, security, safety, audit, or ethics functions.
  4. Use formal channels: escalate to accountable leadership or protected reporting routes when ordinary resolution fails.
  5. Pause, refuse, or report externally where justified: follow applicable law, urgent-safety procedures, contracts, and qualified independent advice.
ESCALATE(concern):
  if imminent serious harm: use the emergency route immediately

  state affected people, relevant duty, severity, reversibility, and urgency
  propose a proportionate test, control, pause, or alternative
  send to an owner with authority; request a decision and deadline
  if unresolved: seek independent review and the next formal channel
  record response, dissent, residual risk, and retaliation or conflict concerns safely
  do not approve work outside your competence or authority
Integrated go / change / pause guide

Proceed with monitoring when evidence matches the intended domain and residual risk has an accountable owner. Change and retest when a lower-risk design can meet the legitimate purpose. Pause and escalate when severe harm, missing authority, invalid consent, prohibited use, material evidence gaps, or ineffective controls remain. “Human in the loop” counts only if the person has information, time, competence, authority, and a real ability to disagree.

Jargon decoder
protected attribute
A characteristic protected under applicable equality law; categories and permitted uses vary by jurisdiction. Removing its column does not remove correlated proxies.
base rate / prevalence
The proportion with observed outcome \(Y=1\) in a defined group and period. Unequal prevalences create specific metric tensions, not a universal impossibility claim.
proxy
A feature correlated with a sensitive or protected characteristic, sometimes reconstructing information the explicit field omitted.
COMPAS
A recidivism-risk tool central to a contested 2016 debate: different analyses emphasized calibration/predictive value versus error-rate disparities.
PII / personal data
PII is a jurisdiction- and policy-dependent term. GDPR “personal data” broadly covers information relating to an identified or identifiable person.
data subject
GDPR's term for the identified or identifiable person to whom personal data relate.
controller / processor
The controller determines purposes and means; a processor acts on the controller's documented instructions, subject to the regulation's precise definitions.
DPIA
Data Protection Impact Assessment—an assessment required under GDPR where processing is likely to result in high risk, with specified content and consultation rules.
pseudonymisation
Processing so data cannot be attributed without separately kept additional information. It reduces some risk but usually remains personal data.
disparate impact
A legal concept concerning disproportionate adverse effects of a facially neutral practice; doctrine and tests vary and are not identical to demographic parity.
residual risk
Risk remaining after controls credited with evidence, including uncertainty about whether those controls work.
dark pattern
An interface design that manipulates or materially impairs a person's autonomous, informed choice.

Self-check

1. A team tried 40 model variants against the final test set and wants to publish only the best score as untouched evaluation. Which response best satisfies integrity of work?

b. Repeated selection on the test set leaks test evidence into model choice. Integrity requires accurate provenance and a new evaluation boundary, not selective presentation.

2. A high-impact hiring model is scheduled tomorrow, but subgroup recall and the appeal workflow are unverified. What is the strongest first professional action?

a. TRACE separates verified gaps from unsupported conclusions, proposes a proportionate remedy, and routes the risk to people with authority and relevant competence.

3. A team removes sex from a hiring model and declares it fair. What is the best technical objection?

c. Representation, labels, proxies, objectives, deployment, and feedback can create disparities. Auditing protected groups may be necessary but must have appropriate legal and privacy governance.

4. Groups A and B each contain 1,000 people. Their prevalences are 60% and 30%, and both have TPR 80% and FPR 20%. Which statement is correct?

d. Shared TPR and FPR satisfy equalized odds. Different prevalence changes the mixture of true and false positives, so selection and PPV differ.

5. Which statement about predictive parity, calibration, and impossibility results is precise?

b. The objects and conditions matter. Universal wording erases perfect, equal-base-rate, and degenerate cases and conflates a thresholded PPV condition with score calibration.

6. A loan-approval label records past officer decisions, and access to officers differed by group. Before optimizing a fairness gap against that label, the team should:

a. Conditional metrics inherit the meaning and defects of \(Y\). The decision context, affected harms, uncertainty, and legal constraints determine which evidence is relevant.

7. Which GDPR summary is most accurate?

c. Lawful basis, rights, and automated-decision rules all require contextual analysis. Pseudonymisation is a safeguard, not automatic anonymisation.

8. An inventory has 100,000 records × 12 fields × 24 months. A justified design needs 5 fields × 6 months. What does the chapter's illustrative exposure calculation show?

d. The arithmetic compares inventory surface only. It does not estimate breach probability or replace purpose, sensitivity, access, legal, and re-identification analysis.

9. As of 18 August 2026, an AI system intended to rank job applicants should initially be treated how under the EU AI Act?

b. Employment uses can fall within Annex III, but classification and current obligations depend on facts and dates. Other privacy, equality, employment, and consumer duties may apply in parallel.

10. A public voice-cloning release has severe impersonation potential, broad reach, easy misuse, and only proposed controls. What best satisfies dual-use and competence duties?

a. High-severity, scalable misuse with weak evidence warrants escalation and staged, tested controls. Competence includes seeking review and declining authority one does not have.
