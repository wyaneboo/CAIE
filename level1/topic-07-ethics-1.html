Professional Ethics & Responsible AI I

Like the CFA's ethics core, this topic is small in hours and disproportionate in consequence — by exam design and by career design. The engineering content is real (the fairness-criteria impossibility below is a theorem, not a sentiment), and the professional content is the difference between an engineer and a chartered one: knowing what you may build, for whom, and when to say no.

Learning outcomes
  • Describe the CAIE Code of Conduct and Standards of Professional Practice.
  • Define algorithmic bias and the major fairness criteria (demographic parity, equalized odds) — and explain why they are mutually incompatible.
  • Describe privacy principles (consent, minimization, purpose limitation) and major regulations (GDPR, EU AI Act risk tiers).
  • Explain informed consent and dual-use risk; describe the duty of competence.

7.1 The Code, compressed

The CAIE Code of Conduct reduces to six standards. Learn them as tests you apply to a scenario:

  1. Integrity of work: report results honestly — no cherry-picked benchmarks, no undisclosed test-set tuning, no claiming a metric you didn't measure.
  2. Duty of competence: operate within the limits of your models and yourself; know your model's failure modes before others discover them; disclose uncertainty.
  3. Duty to users: the people affected by a system outrank the convenience of its builders. Foreseeable harm is your responsibility even when unintended.
  4. Data stewardship: collect the minimum, protect what you hold, use it only for its consented purpose.
  5. Independence and objectivity: resist pressure — commercial or hierarchical — to misstate what a system can do or to skip evaluation.
  6. Professional responsibility: the license to build advanced systems carries the obligation to escalate, document, and — in the limit — refuse (Topic 22 trains this).

7.2 Algorithmic bias and the fairness impossibility

Algorithmic bias is systematic error that disadvantages a group — usually inherited from data (historical decisions encode historical discrimination; sampling under-represents groups; labels proxy something other than truth) or from deployment context. To reason about it, formalize: for a binary predictor \(\hat Y\), outcome \(Y\), and group \(A\), the three canonical criteria are:

  • Demographic parity: \(P(\hat Y = 1 \mid A = a)\) equal across groups — equal selection rates.
  • Equalized odds: \(P(\hat Y = 1 \mid Y = y, A = a)\) equal across groups for each true \(y\) — equal true-positive and false-positive rates.
  • Predictive parity / calibration: \(P(Y = 1 \mid \hat Y = 1, A = a)\) equal — a positive prediction means the same thing for every group.

The theorem the syllabus wants: when base rates differ between groups, no non-perfect classifier can satisfy all of these simultaneously. (Kleinberg et al. 2016; Chouldechova 2016 — provoked by the COMPAS recidivism debate, where the tool was calibrated but had unequal false-positive rates.) This is arithmetic, not politics: choosing which fairness criterion to satisfy is a value judgment about which error costs matter, and it must be made explicitly, documented, and owned — not discovered by journalists.

7.3 Privacy and regulation

The three privacy principles the syllabus names, in engineering terms: consent — data subjects agreed to this use, and consent to use A is not consent to use B (training a model on support tickets collected "to improve service" is a purpose change); minimization — collect and retain only what the purpose requires (every extra column is liability, not asset); purpose limitation — data collected for one purpose cannot be silently repurposed. GDPR operationalizes these for anyone touching EU residents' data, adding rights of access, correction, erasure, and meaningful information about automated decisions — with fines up to 4% of global revenue.

The EU AI Act regulates by risk tier, and the tiers are exam material:

7.4 Consent, dual use, and the duty of competence

Informed consent requires that the person understands what is collected, for what, with what risks, and can refuse without penalty — a checkbox under a 40-page policy is consent-shaped, not consent. Dual-use risk: many capabilities are harm-neutral until context (a voice cloner is an accessibility tool and a fraud tool; a protein-design model is a drug pipeline and a toxin pipeline). The professional duty is to assess plausible misuse before release and mitigate (access controls, monitoring, staged release) — Level III (Topic 19) makes this quantitative.

The duty of competence deserves its physics phrasing: know your model's domain of validity the way you know a Taylor expansion's radius of convergence. A model is a fit to a training distribution; outside it, extrapolation is unlabeled guesswork. The competent engineer can answer: on what data does this fail? for which subgroups is accuracy lowest? how does it behave on out-of-distribution input? — and says "I don't know yet" out loud when that's the truth.

Jargon decoder
protected attribute
A characteristic (race, sex, age, …) legally shielded from discriminatory treatment. Removing the column doesn't remove the signal — proxies (zip code, names) reconstruct it.
base rate
The prevalence of the outcome in a group. Different base rates are what make the fairness criteria collide.
COMPAS
The recidivism-scoring tool at the center of the 2016 ProPublica investigation that made fairness-criteria incompatibility famous.
PII
Personally Identifiable Information — data that can identify a person directly or in combination.
data subject
GDPR's term for the human the data is about — the holder of the rights.
disparate impact
A facially neutral practice with disproportionate adverse effect on a protected group — the legal cousin of demographic-parity violation.

Self-check

1. Two groups have repayment base rates of 60% and 40%. A lender's model is perfectly calibrated for both groups. It follows that:

c. The impossibility theorem: with unequal base rates, calibration and equalized odds are mutually exclusive short of perfection. This is the COMPAS geometry.

2. A team removes "gender" from a hiring model's features and declares it fair. The strongest objection:

b. "Fairness through unawareness" reliably fails — the Amazon hiring-tool case is the canonical example (it learned to penalize the word "women's"). You must measure group outcomes on data where the attribute is available for auditing.

3. Under the EU AI Act, a CV-screening tool that ranks job applicants is:

d. Employment and worker-management uses are explicitly listed high-risk. Ban applies to things like state social scoring; disclosure-only applies to chatbots/deepfakes.

4. Your model ships tomorrow; subgroup evaluation wasn't run; your manager says "we'll check after launch." The Code-consistent move:

a. The professional sequence: document, propose a proportionate remedy, escalate. (c) is the last resort, not the first move — Topic 22 trains the full escalation ladder. (d) is question 2's fallacy.
