Probability, Statistics & Information Theory

Machine learning is statistics executed at industrial scale. Every loss function you will ever minimize is secretly a statement about probability distributions, and this topic is where that secret gets told. For a physicist, the punchline will feel familiar: Shannon's entropy and Gibbs's entropy are the same formula, and training a classifier is minimizing a free energy.

Learning outcomes
  • Apply probability axioms, conditional probability, and Bayes' theorem; work with joint, marginal, and conditional distributions.
  • Describe Bernoulli, Binomial, Gaussian, Poisson, Categorical, Dirichlet distributions and their use cases; compute expectation, variance, covariance, correlation.
  • Explain the CLT and the Law of Large Numbers.
  • Compare frequentist vs Bayesian inference; apply MLE and MAP; construct confidence intervals and hypothesis tests; explain the bias-variance tradeoff formally; describe bootstrapping and cross-validation.
  • Define and compute entropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence, and mutual information; explain why cross-entropy is the natural classification loss; connect MLE to minimizing KL.

2.1 Probability — the algebra of uncertainty

The axioms: probabilities are non-negative, sum to one over the whole sample space, and add for mutually exclusive events. Everything else is bookkeeping. The two workhorses:

Conditional probability \(P(A\mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)\), and Bayes' theorem, which is just conditional probability read in both directions: $$P(H \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid H)\, P(H)}{P(D)} \quad\text{— posterior} = \frac{\text{likelihood} \times \text{prior}}{\text{evidence}}.$$ Bayes is the update rule for beliefs. A classic trap the exam loves: a test with 99% accuracy for a disease with 0.1% prevalence still gives you ≈ 9% chance of disease after a positive result — because the prior is tiny, false positives from the healthy 99.9% swamp the true positives. Base-rate neglect is the #1 probability error working engineers make (it will reappear in Topic 13 when you compute how many drift "alerts" are false alarms).

A joint distribution \(p(x, y)\) contains everything; the marginal \(p(x) = \sum_y p(x,y)\) integrates the rest out; the conditional \(p(y \mid x) = p(x,y)/p(x)\) slices it. Supervised learning is the business of estimating \(p(y \mid x)\); generative modeling (Topic 8) goes after the joint.

Moments. Expectation \(E[X]\) is the average under the distribution; variance \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[(X-\mu)^2]\); covariance \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]\) measures linear co-movement, and correlation is covariance normalized to \([-1,1]\). Independence ⇒ zero covariance, but zero covariance does not imply independence (think \(Y = X^2\)) — a beloved exam trick.

2.2 The Central Limit Theorem and the Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers: sample means converge to the true mean as \(n \to \infty\). It's why evaluation metrics stabilize with more test examples. The Central Limit Theorem is stronger and stranger: the sample mean of any distribution with finite variance becomes Gaussian, $$\bar X_n \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu, \tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\right),$$ regardless of the original shape. That \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) is the error bar on every benchmark number you will ever report — and the reason a 2-point accuracy gain measured on 200 examples (\(\sigma/\sqrt n \approx 3\) points) is noise, not signal.

⚛ Physics bridge

You've met the CLT as the reason a random walk of \(N\) steps spreads as \(\sqrt N\) — diffusion. And the Gaussian's ubiquity is the same fact that makes Maxwell–Boltzmann velocity components Gaussian: many independent microscopic contributions, one universal macroscopic shape. When a distribution has heavy tails (no finite variance — think Lévy flights), the CLT fails, and so do naive error bars; production latency distributions (Topic 20) are exactly this kind of beast, which is why engineers report p95/p99 percentiles instead of means.

2.3 Statistical inference — two philosophies, one toolbox

Frequentist inference treats parameters as fixed unknowns and randomness as living in the data: "if I re-ran this experiment many times, my 95% confidence-interval procedure would trap the true value 95% of the time." Bayesian inference treats parameters as random variables with a prior, and conditions on the one dataset you actually saw: "given this data, the parameter lies in this credible interval with probability 0.95." The frequentist statement is about the procedure; the Bayesian one is about the parameter. Exams punish people who mix them up.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE): choose parameters that maximize the probability of the observed data, \(\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \sum_i \log p(x_i \mid \theta)\). MAP adds the prior: \(\arg\max_\theta \left[\sum_i \log p(x_i\mid\theta) + \log p(\theta)\right]\). That extra term is precisely regularization: a Gaussian prior on weights is L2 weight decay; a Laplace prior is L1. When you tune weight decay in Topic 5, you are choosing a prior width.

Hypothesis testing asks: assuming no effect (the null), how surprising is my data? The p-value is \(P(\text{data at least this extreme} \mid \text{null})\) — not the probability the null is true. You'll use this machinery for real in A/B testing models (Topic 13). Resampling methods sidestep distributional assumptions: bootstrapping re-draws datasets with replacement from your sample to get empirical error bars on any statistic; cross-validation rotates a held-out fold to estimate generalization without wasting data — the default honesty protocol for Topic 4's models.

Bias–variance, formally. For squared error and a model \(\hat f\) trained on a random dataset \(D\): $$E_{D}\big[(y - \hat f_D(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(f(x) - E_D[\hat f_D(x)]\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{E_D\big[(\hat f_D(x) - E_D[\hat f_D(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}.$$ Bias: your model family can't represent the truth (too rigid). Variance: your model dances to the noise of whichever training set it saw (too flexible). You will see this decomposition as a slider in Topic 4's polynomial-fit widget.

2.4 Information theory — surprise, coded in bits

The entropy of a discrete distribution, $$H(P) = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i,$$ is its average surprise — the minimum average number of bits (log base 2) needed to encode samples. Uniform = maximally uncertain = maximal entropy; a point mass = zero entropy. Cross-entropy \(H(P, Q) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i\) is the average bits you pay when reality follows \(P\) but you coded for \(Q\). The overhead is the KL divergence: $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \sum_i p_i \log \frac{p_i}{q_i} = H(P,Q) - H(P) \;\ge 0,$$ zero iff \(P = Q\), and asymmetric — \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|P)\), so it is not a distance. Mutual information \(I(X;Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y))\) measures how far a joint is from independence — the information one variable carries about another, used in feature selection and representation-learning objectives.

Why cross-entropy is the classification loss. Minimizing cross-entropy between the data distribution and your model equals minimizing \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\text{data}} \| Q_\theta)\) (they differ by \(H(P_{\text{data}})\), a constant in \(\theta\)) — and it also equals maximizing likelihood: the empirical average of \(-\log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)\) is the negative log-likelihood. One object, three names. When a language model reports "loss 2.3," that is a cross-entropy in nats per token; perplexity (Topic 9) is just \(e^{2.3} \approx 10\) — "as confused as a 10-sided die."

⚛ Physics bridge

\(H = -\sum p \ln p\) is Gibbs entropy with \(k_B = 1\). Softmax \(p_i \propto e^{z_i/T}\) is the Boltzmann distribution with logits as negative energies — "temperature" in LLM sampling (Topic 9) is exactly thermodynamic temperature. Cross-entropy minimization is free-energy minimization; the log-partition function is log-sum-exp (Topic 1). And maximum entropy subject to constraints — Jaynes's derivation of statistical mechanics — is also how the exponential family of distributions arises in statistics. You already know this subject; it just changed clothes.

Jargon decoder
i.i.d.
Independent and identically distributed — the standard (and often false in production!) assumption that samples come from one fixed distribution. Violations of i.i.d. are called distribution shift (Topic 13).
likelihood
\(p(\text{data}\mid\theta)\) viewed as a function of \(\theta\) with data fixed. Not a probability distribution over θ.
prior / posterior
Belief about parameters before / after seeing data.
nat / bit
Information units: natural log vs log₂. 1 nat ≈ 1.443 bits. Neural losses are usually nats.
perplexity
\(e^{\text{cross-entropy}}\) — effective branching factor; "how many-sided a die is the model as confused as."
conjugate prior
A prior that keeps the posterior in the same family (e.g., Dirichlet is conjugate to Categorical) — algebra convenience, heavily used in Bayesian ML.
p-value
P(data at least this extreme | null hypothesis true). Not the probability the null is true.

Self-check

1. A hallucination detector flags true hallucinations 95% of the time, with a 5% false-positive rate. In your traffic, 2% of answers are hallucinations. An answer gets flagged. Probability it's actually a hallucination?

b. Bayes: (0.95×0.02)/(0.95×0.02 + 0.05×0.98) = 0.019/0.068 ≈ 0.28. The 98% clean answers generate most flags. Every monitoring system you build in Topic 13 lives or dies on this arithmetic.

2. Minimizing cross-entropy loss on classification is equivalent to:

c. H(P,Q) = H(P) + KL(P‖Q), and H(P) doesn't depend on model parameters. Note the direction: it's KL(data‖model) — the direction that punishes assigning tiny probability to real events. (d) is the other direction, which is mode-seeking — relevant to variational inference and RLHF's KL penalty (Topic 17).

3. Your model scores 71.0% vs baseline 69.5% on a 400-example benchmark. Roughly, the standard error on each score is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) ≈ 2.3 points. The right conclusion:

a. CLT gives σ/√n ≈ 2.3 points per score; a 1.5-point gap is ~0.5σ of the difference. (d) is p-hacking. Paired comparisons (same examples, both models) reduce variance and are the professional move.

4. Adding L2 weight decay to a loss is equivalent, in the MAP view, to:

d. \(-\log p(\theta)\) for a Gaussian prior is \(\|\theta\|^2 / 2\sigma^2\) + const — exactly the L2 penalty, with decay strength = 1/σ². Laplace gives L1 (sparsity, Topic 4).
