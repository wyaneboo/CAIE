Deep Learning Fundamentals

A neural network is a stack of matrix multiplications separated by cheap nonlinearities, trained by the chain rule. That single sentence is the whole subject; the other 16% of the exam is the engineering that makes it actually work — initialization, normalization, regularization, and the diagnosis of the many ways training goes wrong.

Learning outcomes
  • Describe the perceptron, MLP, and universal approximation theorem; derive backpropagation for a multi-layer network.
  • Compare activation functions (sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, GELU, SwiGLU) and their gradient behavior; explain Xavier/He initialization.
  • Describe batch/layer/RMS normalization; explain dropout, weight decay, early stopping; describe warmup and cosine-decay schedules.
  • Diagnose training instabilities (NaN losses, dead neurons, exploding gradients).
  • Describe CNNs (convolution, pooling, receptive fields, parameter sharing), RNNs/LSTMs/GRUs and the vanishing-gradient problem, attention at a foundational level, and autoencoders.

5.1 From perceptron to universal approximator

A single perceptron computes \(f(x) = \phi(w^\top x + b)\) — a linear score and a nonlinearity. Alone it can only draw a hyperplane (it famously cannot represent XOR — you proved this to yourself in Topic 4's playground). Stack layers into a multi-layer perceptron: $$h^{(1)} = \phi(W^{(1)} x + b^{(1)}), \quad h^{(2)} = \phi(W^{(2)} h^{(1)} + b^{(2)}), \;\dots$$ and the universal approximation theorem says one sufficiently wide hidden layer can approximate any continuous function on a compact set. Read it correctly: it's an existence theorem. It promises nothing about how many neurons (possibly astronomically many), nothing about whether gradient descent will find the weights, and nothing about generalization. Depth matters in practice because deep compositions represent hierarchical structure exponentially more efficiently than shallow width.

Backpropagation, derived. For a loss \(L\), define at each layer the error signal \(\delta^{(l)} = \partial L / \partial z^{(l)}\) (gradient w.r.t. pre-activations \(z^{(l)} = W^{(l)} h^{(l-1)} + b^{(l)}\)). The chain rule gives the recursion: $$\delta^{(L)} = \nabla_{\hat y} L \odot \phi'(z^{(L)}), \qquad \delta^{(l)} = \big(W^{(l+1)\top} \delta^{(l+1)}\big) \odot \phi'(z^{(l)}),$$ $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(l)}} = \delta^{(l)} \, h^{(l-1)\top}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(l)}} = \delta^{(l)}.$$ Two readings worth internalizing: (1) the forward pass sends activations up, the backward pass sends \(\delta\)s down through the transposed weights — the network run in reverse; (2) each weight's gradient is (its input activation) × (its output's error) — a local product, which is what makes the whole thing \(O(\text{same as forward})\). For softmax + cross-entropy the output error collapses to the beautiful \(\delta^{(L)} = \hat y - y\): predicted minus true.

⚛ Physics bridge

Backprop is the adjoint method — the same trick used in optimal control and in computing sensitivities of ODE solutions: propagate a costate (here \(\delta\)) backwards instead of perturbing every parameter forwards. The vanishing/exploding gradient problem is a product of ~depth-many transfer matrices, i.e., a Lyapunov-exponent question: unless the typical Jacobian spectral norm is pinned near 1, the signal grows or decays exponentially in depth. Xavier/He initialization, normalization layers, and residual connections are all devices for holding that Lyapunov exponent at zero — training networks at the edge of chaos.

5.2 The nonlinearity zoo and why initialization matters

Sigmoid \(1/(1+e^{-z})\) squashes to (0,1); its gradient maxes at 0.25 and dies in the tails — stacking sigmoids multiplies many numbers < 0.25, hence classical nets deeper than a few layers wouldn't train. tanh is the zero-centered fix, same saturation issue. ReLU \(\max(0,z)\) has gradient exactly 1 on the positive side — no decay through active units — at the price of dead neurons: a unit pushed permanently negative gets zero gradient forever. GELU \(z\,\Phi(z)\) is the smooth ReLU used by transformers. SwiGLU is a gated feed-forward variant used in Llama-class models: \(\mathrm{Swish}(W_1 x) \odot W_3 x\) — one branch computes values, the other decides how much passes, which is empirically a stronger FFN at equal parameters.

Initialization. If weights start too large, activations and gradients explode layer by layer; too small, they vanish. Set the variance so the signal's scale is preserved: Xavier/Glorot \(\mathrm{Var}(W) = 2/(n_{in}+n_{out})\) for tanh-like activations; He \(\mathrm{Var}(W) = 2/n_{in}\) for ReLU (the factor 2 compensates for ReLU zeroing half its inputs). Get this wrong by 2× per layer and a 50-layer net sees \(2^{50}\).

5.3 Training deep networks — the stabilizer stack

Normalization. BatchNorm standardizes each channel using the statistics of the current minibatch, then rescales with learned γ, β. It smooths optimization dramatically but couples examples within a batch and behaves differently at train vs inference time (running statistics) — a classic source of bugs. LayerNorm normalizes across features within each example — batch-size independent, hence the default in transformers. RMSNorm drops the mean-centering and just divides by the root-mean-square — cheaper, works as well, used in Llama-class models. Where the normalization sits (pre-LN vs post-LN) matters for stability — that story continues in Topic 8.

Regularization. Dropout randomly zeroes each unit with probability p during training (scaling the survivors), forcing redundancy — you can read it as training an implicit ensemble of subnetworks. Weight decay is the L2/Gaussian-prior story from Topics 2 and 4 (in Adam, done correctly it's "decoupled" — AdamW). Early stopping halts when validation loss turns upward: capacity control via training time itself.

Learning-rate schedules. Warmup ramps the LR from ~0 over the first few hundred/thousand steps: early on, Adam's second-moment estimates are noisy and normalization statistics unsettled, so full-size steps at t=0 can fling the weights into bad regions. Cosine decay then glides the LR down smoothly to near zero — large steps early to cross the landscape, small steps late to settle into a minimum. The pair (linear warmup → cosine decay) is the de-facto standard recipe for pretraining.

5.4 Core architectures — matching structure to data

CNNs. A convolution slides one small kernel across the image, computing dot products. Two ideas do all the work: parameter sharing (the same edge detector is useful everywhere — translation symmetry baked into the architecture) and locality (nearby pixels matter most). Stacking layers grows the receptive field — the input patch a deep unit can see — so early layers learn edges, middle layers textures and parts, late layers objects. Pooling (or strided conv) downsamples, buying translation tolerance and cheaper compute. A conv layer with a k×k kernel, C_in→C_out channels costs k²·C_in·C_out parameters — independent of image size, which is the entire trick versus a dense layer.

RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs. A recurrent net processes a sequence by folding each token into a hidden state: \(h_t = \phi(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t)\). Backprop through time multiplies by \(W_h\)'s Jacobian once per timestep — the vanishing/exploding gradient problem in its purest form: long-range dependencies decay exponentially. The LSTM fixes this with an additive cell state — a conveyor belt modified only by learned gates (forget, input, output), so gradients can flow along it un-multiplied; the GRU is the cheaper two-gate version. This "protected additive path" is the same design principle as residual connections and is why you should see LSTMs and ResNets as siblings.

Attention, the preview. RNNs force all history through one fixed-size state. Attention instead lets each position directly retrieve from every other: compare a query against all keys, softmax the similarities into weights, take the weighted sum of values. No distance decay, perfect parallelism — and quadratic cost. Topic 8 devotes itself to this mechanism; here you need only the intuition: attention is a differentiable soft dictionary lookup.

Autoencoders squeeze the input through a low-dimensional bottleneck and reconstruct it: encoder \(z = f(x)\), decoder \(\hat x = g(z)\), loss \(\|x - \hat x\|^2\). The bottleneck \(z\) — the latent space — is forced to keep only what matters; a linear autoencoder provably recovers PCA's subspace, so think of autoencoders as nonlinear PCA. Latents become the currency of generative modeling (VAEs and latent diffusion, Topics 8 and 10).
