Professional Standards & Ethics III

Level I gave you the Code; Level II gave you the audit tools. Level III hands you the cases where the Code doesn't obviously apply — where two duties conflict, the facts are incomplete, your employer is pushing, and "escalate to someone else" isn't available because you are the someone else. This final topic is deliberately last: it assumes every technical topic before it, because the hardest ethical calls are also technical calls — you can't weigh a harm you can't measure, and you can't defend a refusal you can't argue quantitatively. The chartered engineer's distinguishing mark is not knowing the rules; it is reasoning well when the rules run out — and being willing to say no with your signature on the reasons.

Learning outcomes
  • Apply the full Code and Standards to ambiguous, multi-duty cases.
  • Resolve conflicts among business pressure, user welfare, and professional duty — with a defensible process.
  • Evaluate societal externalities: labor displacement, misuse, environmental cost.
  • Defend, in writing, a decision not to build or deploy a system.

22.1 When duties conflict — the deliberation method

Easy ethics questions have a villain. Real ones have a schedule: a launch date, a revenue target, a competitor shipping first, and a genuine benefit to users on the other side of the ledger. The method that survives ambiguity is procedural — graders (and courts, and postmortems) evaluate the process, because the outcome is often uncertain either way:

  1. Establish the facts, and their uncertainty. What do you actually know about the harm — measured, suspected, or rumored? Much apparent ethical disagreement is factual disagreement in disguise; your first duty is the Level II skill of measuring (audit, evals, Topics 15, 19).
  2. Name every stakeholder and duty in play. Users, affected non-users, employer, profession, public. Duties: competence, honesty, user welfare, confidentiality, legality. Write the actual conflict as "duty A to X vs duty B to Y" — most cases become tractable once stated precisely.
  3. Generate the third options. "Ship it" vs "refuse" is almost never the real choice set: staged rollout with monitoring, scope reduction, human-in-the-loop, disclosure, independent review, delay-with-milestones. The strongest answers expand the option space before choosing.
  4. Decide with the asymmetry of harms in view. Reversible, detectable, compensable harms tolerate more risk; irreversible or rights-violating harms tolerate almost none (the same asymmetry as Topic 19's deployment logic). Who bears the risk, and did they consent?
  5. Document, disclose, and set your escalation path. The decision memo — facts, options, reasoning, dissent — protects users first and you second. Know in advance what evidence triggers re-opening the decision, and what triggers escalation (up, to the board; out, to a regulator, as the last resort with its own high bar).
⚛ Physics bridge

Treat an ethical judgment like a measurement with error bars, not a proof. You report a best estimate and its uncertainty, you state your systematics (incentives are systematic error — yours included: your bonus, your deadline, your sunk pride in the system), and you design the decision so that being wrong is survivable. A physicist who reports a value without error bars has failed professionally even if the value is right; an engineer who ships a judgment without stating its uncertainty and reversal conditions has failed the same way.

Interactive — ambiguous-case deliberation trainer
Four cases with no villain and no clean answer. Commit to a course of action; the grader notes evaluate your reasoning against the deliberation method — including the defensible aspects of the options you didn't pick.

Things to notice: (1) In every case the best answer is a process — measure, expand options, stage, document — not a heroic yes or no. (2) The wrong answers aren't evil; they're shortcuts: skipping the facts, collapsing the option space, or letting the deadline decide. (3) Case 4 is the "defend a no" essay in miniature — note how the strong answer refuses specifically, with conditions under which the answer changes.

22.2 Externalities — the costs that don't appear on your dashboard

An externality is a cost (or benefit) borne by someone outside the transaction: your users click, your company profits, and someone who never touched the product pays. Level III expects you to estimate them — roughly, with stated assumptions, the way you estimated FLOPs and dollars (Topics 12, 20) — because "we considered the societal impact" without numbers is decoration. The three the syllabus names:

  • Labor displacement. Automation rarely deletes "jobs"; it deletes tasks, and reshapes jobs around what remains. The honest analysis asks: which tasks, what share of whose working hours, how fast (speed determines whether adaptation is possible), and does the deployment complement workers (raising their output) or substitute for them? The same system can be either, depending on design choices you control — drafting-with-review versus full replacement, for instance.
  • Misuse. Capability you ship is capability someone else aims (dual use, Topic 7). The professional questions: what does your system make cheaper for a bad actor (scale, skill, anonymity)? What's the marginal uplift over what's already available? Which controls — access tiers, rate limits, monitoring, KYC — cut misuse without destroying legitimate use? (This is Topic 19's dangerous-capability framing applied to ordinary products.)
  • Environmental cost. Training is a one-time megawatt bill; inference at scale is the recurring one (a billion queries a day at ~0.3–3 Wh each is a town's worth of electricity). Estimate energy × grid carbon intensity, honestly account for location and efficiency choices you control, and weigh it like any other cost — neither ignored nor treated as automatically disqualifying.
Interactive — the externality ledger
A back-of-envelope social accounting for a deployment: set the scale and design choices, read off rough labor, misuse, and carbon estimates — and see which mitigation levers move each line. Assumptions shown; argue with them.

Things to try: (1) Slide human-review from 0% to 100%: the displaced-hours line converts into "augmented hours" — the complement-vs-substitute choice is a design parameter, not fate. (2) At a billion queries/day, even 1 Wh/query is ~150 GWh/yr; move the grid slider from coal-heavy (0.8) to clean (0.05) and watch the carbon line — siting and efficiency are engineering decisions with externality-sized consequences. (3) The ledger produces no verdict. That's the point: it produces the numbers your judgment has to answer to.

22.3 Defending the "no" — the hardest deliverable

The syllabus's final learning outcome is the profession's reason for existing: sometimes the right engineering output is a well-argued refusal. A defensible "no" is not moral posturing — it is a technical document, and it has a structure:

  1. State what was asked, and the legitimate interest behind it. Steelman the request. A "no" that misrepresents the ask persuades no one and deserves to fail.
  2. State the specific harm, with evidence and mechanism. Not "this feels wrong," but which people, harmed how, at what estimated scale and reversibility — using your audit, eval, and externality numbers (Topics 15, 19, 22.2). Where evidence is incomplete, say what you'd measure and why proceeding-before-measuring is itself the risk.
  3. Show the alternatives you tried to make "yes" work. The scope reductions, safeguards, and staged options you generated (§22.1 step 3) and why each fails to contain the harm. A "no" is credible in proportion to the "yes" attempts that preceded it.
  4. State the conditions under which the answer changes. A professional refusal is falsifiable: "with consent flows X and audit results Y, I would revisit." An unconditional "no" is a position; a conditional one is engineering.
  5. Accept the cost, and put your name on it. In writing, with the dissent recorded even if overruled. If overruled and the harm is grave and irreversible, the escalation ladder of §22.1 applies — and, at its end, the willingness to walk away is what makes every earlier step credible.

Notice this is the same skeleton as Topic 19's deployment decision and Topic 21's capstone tradeoff — constraint, evidence, options, decision rule, ownership. By design: the ethics essay is a systems-design essay whose failure mode is measured in people.

22.4 The Code, complete — what "chartered" finally means

Across three levels the Code has scaled with your capability, and that is the pattern to carry out of this course: duty grows with power. At Level I it bound your claims (competence, honesty about what your model can do). At Level II it bound your builds (audit what you ship, document what you know, protect what users gave you). At Level III it binds your judgment: the systems you approve act at a scale where your professional yes or no is itself an instrument of consequence. The charter is not a credential that certifies you know the material; it is a public promise about how you will behave when the material runs out — when the case is ambiguous, the pressure is real, and the only enforcement in the room is you.

Jargon decoder
externality
A cost or benefit borne by someone outside the transaction — the deployment's unpriced effects on non-users.
complement vs substitute
Technology that raises a worker's output vs replaces the worker; often a design choice within the same system.
task vs job displacement
Automation removes tasks; jobs restructure around the remainder — the honest unit of labor analysis.
marginal uplift
How much easier your system makes a harm relative to existing alternatives — the misuse question that matters.
steelman
Presenting the opposing case in its strongest form before arguing against it; the credibility precondition of a refusal.
conditional refusal
A "no" with stated conditions that would change it — falsifiable, hence professional.
escalation ladder
The ordered path when overruled: document dissent → escalate internally → external report (last resort, high bar) → walk away.
moral crumple zone
Designing a human "in the loop" who absorbs blame without real authority or time to intervene — oversight theater to name and refuse.

Self-check

1. Your fraud model would gain 2% precision by adding a feature you suspect proxies for a protected attribute. Legal says it's technically compliant; the audit is inconclusive; launch is Friday. The strongest next move:

c. The third option the deadline hid: the launch and the feature are separable. It resolves the facts before the risk is taken (§22.1 steps 1, 3), keeps the decision reversible, and documents everything. (a) takes an irreversible reputational/discrimination risk on inconclusive facts; (b) skips every internal step; (d) violates honesty and documentation duties outright.

2. A hospital deployment will have nurses "review" AI triage notes — but the rollout plan gives them 20 seconds each and holds them accountable for errors. Ethically, this design is:

b. Human-in-the-loop is a safety mechanism only if the human can genuinely catch and stop errors; otherwise it's a liability-routing device. The chartered move is to name it and fix the design — real review time, authority to override, and accountability that sits with the system's owners, not its operators.

3. Estimating your product's labor externality, the defensible analysis unit is:

d. §22.2: automation operates on tasks, and speed determines whether adaptation is possible. The complement-vs-substitute lever is partly in the engineer's hands — which is exactly why it's a professional duty and not (b) someone else's problem.

4. You've concluded a requested system shouldn't be built. Which refusal earns the marks (and deserves to)?

a. The §22.3 structure: a falsifiable, evidenced, alternatives-exhausted, signed refusal. (b) may be right but is undefendable and easily dismissed; (c) is dishonesty by schedule; (d) abandons the duty the charter exists to enforce. The exam — and the profession — grades the reasoning you're willing to sign.
