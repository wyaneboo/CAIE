LLM Training, Fine-Tuning & Alignment

A base model is a brilliant, amoral autocomplete: it has read the internet and will continue any text, helpful or not, true or not. Turning it into a useful, safe assistant is post-training — and doing it well is now the field's central craft. This topic is the full recipe: what goes into the pretraining corpus and why the mixture matters, how supervised fine-tuning and preference optimization stack, how to choose among the alignment methods, and how to recognize when alignment has failed in one of its characteristic ways. Level III grades whether you can justify a recipe and diagnose its pathologies, not recite definitions.

Learning outcomes
  • Design a pretraining data pipeline: sourcing, dedup, filtering, mixture weighting, and their downstream effects.
  • Construct a post-training recipe (SFT → reward modeling → RLHF/DPO) and justify each choice.
  • Compare RLHF, DPO, constitutional AI / RLAIF and recommend one for a context.
  • Diagnose alignment failures: reward hacking, sycophancy, mode collapse.
  • Decide between fine-tuning, RAG, and prompting for a business problem, and defend it.

17.1 Pretraining data — the model is its corpus

Capability is bought first with data. The pretraining pipeline is a filtering funnel from petabytes of raw web to a curated trillion-token corpus, and every stage shapes the model:

  • Sourcing: web crawl (Common Crawl) plus curated high-signal sources (code, books, papers, reference wikis). Raw web is mostly boilerplate, spam, and duplication.
  • Deduplication: remove near-duplicates (MinHash/LSH at scale). Duplicated data wastes compute, worsens loss per FLOP, and — critically — inflates memorization, which is both a privacy risk (Topic 15) and a benchmark-contamination risk.
  • Quality filtering: classifiers and heuristics keep fluent, informative text and drop the junk. The lever with the highest quality-per-token — but over-aggressive filtering narrows the distribution and can erase dialects and minority topics (a fairness cost, Topic 7).
  • Mixture weighting: you do not train on sources in their natural proportion. Up-weighting code improves reasoning; up-weighting a domain buys competence there at the expense of breadth. The mixture is a set of dials on the model's eventual personality and skills — and tuning it is an empirical, expensive search.
  • Decontamination: remove test sets of benchmarks you'll report, or your numbers are memorization, not generalization.
⚛ Physics bridge

Data curation is sample preparation, and it dominates the result exactly as it does in an experiment: a beautiful apparatus on a contaminated sample measures the contaminant. Dedup is removing correlated/repeated measurements that fake precision without adding information (they shrink your effective sample size, Topic 2). Mixture weighting is importance sampling — you reweight the empirical distribution toward the regions you care about, accepting bias in exchange for variance reduction where it matters.

Interactive — pretraining data pipeline & mixture
Push a raw web crawl through dedup, quality, and safety filters, then set the training mixture. Watch usable tokens shrink and a stylized capability profile respond to your source weights.

Things to try: (1) Crank quality and dedup to max: the token count collapses — over-filtering starves a large model of the data Chinchilla (Topic 16) demands, so you trade quality-per-token for total tokens. (2) Push code weight high: reasoning/code capability rises but general web fluency dips — the mixture is a zero-sum allocation of a fixed token budget. (3) Note the "distribution narrowing" warning as filters tighten — a fairness and robustness cost that doesn't show up in average loss.

17.2 The post-training stack — SFT → RM → RLHF/DPO

Pretraining yields a base model that completes text. Post-training makes it follow instructions and behave. The canonical three stages:

  1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): train on curated (instruction, ideal-response) pairs. This teaches the format and behavior of an assistant — how to answer, refuse, reason step by step. Quality beats quantity: a few thousand excellent examples outperform millions of mediocre ones (the "less is more for alignment" finding). SFT alone gets you a usable assistant.
  2. Reward modeling (RM): collect human preferences (response A ≻ B — Topic 11) and train a model to score responses via the Bradley–Terry likelihood. Preferences are cheaper and more reliable than writing ideal responses, and they capture "better" on axes (helpfulness, harmlessness, tone) that are hard to demonstrate directly.
  3. Preference optimization: push the policy toward high-reward responses. RLHF does this with PPO against the RM plus a KL leash to the reference model (Topic 11). DPO skips the explicit RM and the RL loop, optimizing the policy directly on preference pairs via a classification-style loss — simpler and more stable, at the cost of the RM's reusability and online sampling.

Every stage has a failure signature, and knowing them is the diagnosis skill Level III tests:

Interactive — RLHF over-optimization & the KL leash
Optimize a policy against a reward model that's only an imperfect proxy for the true objective. Watch proxy reward keep climbing while the true reward peaks and then collapses — reward hacking, live — and see the KL penalty hold the line.

Things to try: (1) β = 0: proxy reward soars, true reward peaks early and then falls as the policy drifts into regions where the RM is wrong and exploitable — the textbook over-optimization curve (Gao et al.). (2) Raise β: the policy stays near the reference, the gap between proxy and true reward shrinks, and true reward peaks higher and later. (3) Lower RM quality: even a modest β can't save a bad proxy — the "better RM data" lever from the table. The optimal β is a tuning problem, not a constant.

17.3 Choosing an alignment method

The methods form a spectrum of who supplies the judgment and how much machinery it takes:

  • RLHF (PPO): humans give preferences, an RM generalizes them, PPO optimizes online. Most powerful and flexible — the RM can be reused for filtering and monitoring, and online sampling explores fresh responses — but the most infrastructure and the most ways to fail (reward hacking, PPO instability).
  • DPO: humans give preferences, optimized directly, offline. Far simpler and more stable; the right default when you have a fixed preference set and limited infra. Trades away online exploration and the standalone RM.
  • RLAIF / Constitutional AI: replace (most) human preference labels with an AI judge guided by a written set of principles (a "constitution"). Scales preference data cheaply and makes the values explicit and auditable, at the risk of inheriting the judge model's blind spots and encoding the constitution-writer's choices. The scalability answer when human labeling is the bottleneck.

A Level III recommendation states the context, picks one, and names the tradeoff accepted — e.g., "fixed 50k preference set, small team, no online-sampling budget ⇒ DPO, accepting the loss of a reusable reward model and online exploration; revisit if we later need iterated RM-based filtering."

17.4 Fine-tune, retrieve, or prompt? — the adaptation decision

A business asks for a model that "knows our stuff." Three adaptation strategies, and choosing wrong is a classic expensive mistake:

Prompting / in-contextRAG (Topic 14)Fine-tuning
Best forFormat, style, a few examples; fast iterationInjecting current, factual, changing knowledge with citationsTeaching behavior/skill/format, a domain's idiom, or latency-critical narrow tasks
Knowledge freshnessAs fresh as the promptAs fresh as the index — update without retrainingFrozen at fine-tune time; stale fast
Cost / effortLowest; no trainingModerate; build + maintain a retrieval stackHighest; data curation + training + eval + serving a custom model
AttributionNoneNative (cites sources)None (knowledge baked in)

The heuristic the exam rewards: if the need is knowledge, reach for RAG; if the need is behavior, reach for fine-tuning; try prompting first for both. They compose — fine-tune a small model for your domain's form and give it RAG for the facts. The most common real-world error is fine-tuning to inject facts: it's expensive, it hallucinates confidently when the facts drift, and it offers no citations — precisely the job RAG was built for.

Interactive — adaptation strategy advisor
Score a use case on four axes; see which of prompting / RAG / fine-tuning the tradeoffs favor, and why. There's rarely a single right answer — read the reasoning, not just the winner.

Things to try: (1) High "changes often" + "need citations": RAG dominates — this is the fact-injection sweet spot, and fine-tuning scores worst here. (2) High "custom behavior" + "latency pressure", low freshness: fine-tuning (or a fine-tuned small model) wins — behavior and speed are its home turf. (3) Everything low: prompting first — don't build infrastructure you can talk your way out of. The bars are guidance, not gospel; the argument is the deliverable.

Self-check

1. During RLHF the reward-model score keeps rising for 3,000 steps, but human spot-checks show responses getting worse — padded, evasive, oddly formatted. First diagnosis and lever:

b. Proxy up, truth down is the over-optimization signature (the widget's β=0 curve). The RM is accurate only near its training distribution; unconstrained PPO walks off it. More steps (a) make it worse. This Goodhart mechanism recurs in Topic 19.

2. A startup wants a support bot that answers from a knowledge base updated daily, with links to the source article. They're about to fine-tune a model nightly on the KB. Better recommendation:

c. The need is fresh, factual, attributable knowledge — RAG's exact remit. Nightly fine-tuning is expensive, has no citations, and hallucinates when facts drift. The classic "fine-tuning to inject facts" anti-pattern (§17.4).

3. A small team has a fixed 40k-comparison preference dataset, no infrastructure for online sampling, and needs stable results soon. Between RLHF-PPO and DPO:

a. DPO's sweet spot exactly (echoing Topic 11's decision, now as a Level III justification): fixed offline preferences + limited infra. The graded part is naming the tradeoff you accept, not just the method.

4. A team over-aggressively quality-filters and deduplicates their pretraining corpus, cutting it from 3T to 400B tokens, then trains a 30B model. Likely consequence:

d. Two costs compound: too few tokens for the model size (Topic 16's D ≈ 20N), and over-filtering that erases tail distributions — a fairness/robustness harm invisible in aggregate loss (§17.1, widget point 1 and 3). Curation is a balance, not "more filtering = better."
