Production Reliability, Governance & Economics

A model that is accurate, aligned, and fast is still a failure if it falls over under load, costs more to run than it earns, or can't be audited when a regulator asks. This topic is the business and operations reality of AI at scale — the reliability engineering that keeps it up, the unit economics that keep it solvent, and the governance that keeps it lawful and accountable. It carries the joint-highest weight in Level III because it is where senior engineers spend their judgment: translating "make it reliable and cheap" into SLOs, error budgets, dollars per token, and an incident process — with the math shown.

Learning outcomes
  • Design SLOs/SLAs and error budgets for AI systems.
  • Architect for reliability: fallbacks, circuit breakers, graceful degradation, rate limiting.
  • Model inference economics: cost per token, utilization, batching, caching, build-vs-buy.
  • Optimize a serving stack for cost under a latency constraint, with the math.
  • Design governance: model risk management, audit trails, approval workflows, compliance.
  • Construct an incident-response and postmortem process for AI-specific failures.

20.1 SLOs, SLAs, and error budgets

Reliability is a target you choose, not a virtue you maximize — 100% is the wrong goal (infinitely expensive, and users can't tell 99.99% from 100%). The SRE vocabulary:

  • SLI (indicator): a measured quantity — availability, p99 latency, error rate, or an AI-specific one like answer-quality or groundedness rate (Topics 13–14).
  • SLO (objective): the internal target for an SLI — "99.9% of requests succeed under 500 ms, measured monthly."
  • SLA (agreement): the external, contractual promise, always looser than the SLO so you have margin before you owe refunds.
  • Error budget: the allowed unreliability, \(1 - \text{SLO}\). At 99.9% monthly that is ~43 minutes of downtime a month. This is the topic's key idea: the budget is a resource to spend — on shipping features, on risky deploys — and when it's exhausted, the policy is to stop shipping and fix reliability. It turns the eternal dev-vs-ops fight into arithmetic.
⚛ Physics bridge

The error budget is a tolerance budget, exactly as in an experiment: you don't demand zero error from every component, you allocate a total error and spend it where it's cheapest to control. And the "nines" are a log scale — each additional nine costs roughly an order of magnitude more engineering for an order-of-magnitude smaller absolute gain, the classic diminishing return. Chasing 99.999% when users can't perceive past 99.9% is spending precision where the instrument can't read it.

Interactive — the error-budget calculator
Pick an availability SLO and see the downtime it permits, how a dependency chain erodes it, and how much redundancy buys back. The "nines" are less generous than they sound.

Things to try: (1) A "four nines" component sounds bulletproof, but chain four of them in series and availability multiplies down — the whole is always less reliable than its weakest part. (2) Add redundancy (parallel replicas): each dependency's failure probability squares or cubes, dragging the chain back up. (3) Read the monthly error budget in minutes: 99.9% ≈ 43 min, 99.99% ≈ 4.3 min. That budget is what you're spending every time you deploy on a Friday.

20.2 Architecting for reliability

You spend the error budget wisely by designing so that failures degrade rather than cascade. The standard patterns, all directly applicable to LLM serving:

  • Fallbacks: when the primary path fails, serve a degraded-but-useful alternative — a smaller/cheaper model, a cached answer, or a graceful "I can't do that right now." For LLMs: fall back from a frontier model to a fast one, or from generation to retrieval-only.
  • Circuit breakers: when a dependency is failing, stop calling it for a cool-down instead of piling on retries that deepen the outage (and drive up cost). The breaker trips on an error-rate threshold, opens, then half-opens to test recovery — borrowed straight from electrical engineering.
  • Graceful degradation: shed non-essential work under load — drop re-ranking (Topic 14), shorten context, disable an expensive tool — to keep the core service alive. Partial service beats none.
  • Rate limiting & load shedding: protect the system from traffic spikes and abusive callers; when saturated, reject early and cheaply (return a 429) rather than accepting work you'll drop late — recall Topic 13's queueing: latency diverges near capacity, so admission control is a reliability tool, not just a billing one.
  • Timeouts & retries with backoff: bounded waits and exponential backoff with jitter, so a blip doesn't become a synchronized retry storm (the thundering herd).

20.3 Inference economics — the unit that decides the business

For most deployed LLM systems, inference is the dominant recurring cost, and the CFO-facing unit is cost per token (or per request). Build the number from the hardware: $$\text{cost/token} = \frac{\text{GPU \$/hour}}{3600 \times \text{throughput (tokens/s)} \times \text{utilization}}.$$ Every reliability and performance lever moves one of those terms, and the tradeoffs are the heart of the Level III cost essay:

  • Batching (Topic 13) raises throughput and utilization by amortizing fixed overhead — the single biggest cost lever — at the price of latency. Continuous/in-flight batching (vLLM) keeps the GPU full as requests come and go.
  • Caching: the KV-cache (Topic 8) within a request; prompt/prefix caching across requests that share a long system prompt (compute it once); and a semantic cache that returns a stored answer for a near-duplicate query — each converts compute into a cheap lookup.
  • Model choice & compression: a distilled/quantized smaller model (Topic 13) at a fraction of the cost per token — routed to only when the frontier model isn't needed (a model router, a bandit in business clothes, Topic 11).
  • Utilization: idle GPUs bleed money; autoscaling, spot/preemptible capacity, and traffic shaping keep the denominator high.

Build vs buy is the recurring decision: an API (pay per token, zero ops, fast to start, price and data-control at the vendor's mercy) versus self-hosting (high fixed cost and operational burden, but low marginal cost at volume and full control). The crossover is a break-even calculation on your volume — below it, buy; well above it, building pays back. Show the arithmetic; a Level III answer that asserts "self-host to save money" without the break-even point earns nothing.

Interactive — serving-stack cost optimizer
Meet a latency SLO at the lowest cost per million tokens. Turn the levers — batch size, prefix/semantic caching, quantization, model tier — and watch cost and p99 latency trade against your SLO line.

Things to try: (1) Raise batch size: cost/token falls steeply (utilization ↑) while p99 climbs — push it until you're just under the SLO line, the cost-optimal operating point. (2) Caching is nearly free money: a 50% prefix-cache hit on a long shared system prompt roughly halves prefill cost with no latency penalty. (3) Drop to a 7B INT4 model and the cost collapses — but only deploy it where quality allows; the honest optimizer routes easy queries here and hard ones to the frontier model.

20.4 Governance, risk, and compliance

At scale, "we tested it" is not enough; you need governance — the processes that make AI decisions accountable, auditable, and lawful. The components a Level III answer should name:

  • Model risk management (MRM): a formal lifecycle — models are inventoried, risk-tiered, independently validated before deployment, and periodically re-reviewed. Borrowed from banking (SR 11-7), now standard for high-stakes AI.
  • Audit trails & lineage: who deployed which model version, trained on what data, approved by whom, and every consequential decision it made — the record a regulator or a court will demand (ties to Topic 13's registry and Topic 7's legal duties).
  • Approval workflows: deployments (and, for agents, consequential actions — Topic 18) pass through defined sign-offs proportionate to risk. High-risk changes need more eyes, not fewer.
  • Regulatory compliance: mapping obligations (EU AI Act risk tiers, GDPR, sector rules) to concrete controls — documentation (model cards/datasheets, Topic 15), human oversight, transparency, and data rights. The responsible scaling policies of Topic 19 are governance for capability risk specifically.

20.5 Incident response and the blameless postmortem

Things will break; maturity is measured by the response. The lifecycle — detect (monitoring/alerting, Topic 13) → triage/mitigate (rollback, fallback, circuit-break) → resolve → learn — ends where the value is: the blameless postmortem. Blameless is a design choice, not niceness: people who fear punishment hide the information you need to prevent recurrence, so you fix systems and processes, not people. A good postmortem states impact, timeline, root cause, and — the point — concrete action items with owners.

AI adds failure modes classical postmortems don't cover, and naming them is the topic's synthesis: silent quality degradation (no error thrown — drift, Topic 13), non-deterministic and hard-to-reproduce incidents (agent traces, Topic 18), training-serving skew and data-pipeline breaks (Topic 13), prompt-injection and jailbreak incidents (Topics 15, 18, 19), and upstream model/provider changes that shift behavior beneath you. The AI-specific incident runbook and its rehearsed rollback are themselves a Level III deliverable.

Jargon decoder
SLI / SLO / SLA
Measured indicator / internal target / external contractual promise (SLA looser than SLO for margin).
error budget
1 − SLO: the allowed unreliability, spent on features/risk; exhausting it freezes launches until reliability recovers.
the nines
99.9% ≈ 43 min/month down; 99.99% ≈ 4.3 min. Each nine costs ~10× more engineering.
circuit breaker
Stops calling a failing dependency for a cool-down (open → half-open → closed) to prevent cascades and retry storms.
graceful degradation
Shedding non-essential work under load to keep the core service alive; partial service beats none.
load shedding / rate limiting
Rejecting excess traffic early (429) to stay left of the latency cliff; admission control as reliability.
cost per token
The unit economic: GPU $/hr ÷ (throughput × utilization); batching, caching, and model choice move the terms.
prefix / semantic cache
Reuse a shared prompt's compute / return a stored answer for a near-duplicate query — compute → cheap lookup.
build vs buy
Self-host (high fixed, low marginal, full control) vs API (zero ops, pay-per-token); a break-even on your volume.
model risk management
Formal inventory, risk-tiering, independent validation, and periodic review of deployed models.
blameless postmortem
Incident review that fixes systems not people, so information isn't hidden; ends in owned action items.

Self-check

1. Your product SLO is 99.9% monthly availability. The team has already burned the whole error budget by the 20th of the month on a series of risky deploys. Per error-budget policy, the right call is:

c. The error budget converts the reliability-vs-velocity argument into a rule: budget remaining → ship; budget spent → stop and stabilize. (b) and (d) are gaming the target, not managing it; (a) discards the mechanism's whole value.

2. A downstream provider's API starts timing out intermittently. Your service keeps retrying aggressively, which worsens the provider's overload and spikes your costs and latency. The pattern you're missing:

b. Aggressive retries against a failing dependency are a classic cascade amplifier (the retry storm). The breaker cuts the feedback loop; backoff+jitter desynchronizes retries; a fallback keeps you serving. (d) is the anti-pattern that caused it.

3. Which change usually gives the largest cost-per-token reduction for a throughput-bound LLM service, and what's the price?

d. Batching amortizes fixed per-forward overhead across many requests, the biggest lever on utilization and thus cost (Topics 13, 20.3). The tradeoff is latency — you push batch size up to the SLO line, not past it. (c) is never the answer.

4. A model's answer quality quietly degrades over two weeks after an upstream provider silently updated the base model; no errors, no latency change, no alerts. The postmortem should conclude:

a. This is the AI-specific failure of §20.5: no error is thrown, so system monitoring is blind — you need quality SLIs and drift detection (Topic 13), version pinning, and a tested rollback. Blame (b) suppresses the information a good postmortem needs.
