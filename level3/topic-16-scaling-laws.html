Scaling Laws & Frontier System Architecture

Level III stops asking you to build and starts asking you to decide — and to defend the decision in writing against a rubric, CFA-essay style. This topic is where the money is spent: a frontier training run is a multi-million-dollar bet placed before the run, justified entirely by an empirical regularity. Scaling laws are that regularity — the closest thing ML has to a law of physics: loss falls as a clean power law in compute, data, and parameters, over ten orders of magnitude. Knowing how to allocate a fixed compute budget across model size and data, and when to spend it on a sparse (MoE) architecture instead, is the core competence here.

Learning outcomes
  • State and apply scaling laws; use the Chinchilla rule to allocate a fixed FLOPs budget.
  • Reason about the compute / data / parameter tradeoff, including inference-aware ("over-training") deviations.
  • Describe Mixture-of-Experts: routing, load balancing, sparse activation, and its costs.
  • Explain long-context techniques and their architectural price.
  • Design and defend an architecture for a target capability, latency, and hardware budget.

16.1 The scaling laws — loss as a power law

The empirical finding (Kaplan et al. 2020; refined by Hoffmann et al. 2022, "Chinchilla"): test loss of a transformer is well fit by a power law in each of model parameters \(N\), training tokens \(D\), and compute \(C\), as long as the other two aren't bottlenecks. The Chinchilla parametric form: $$L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}$$ with \(\alpha \approx \beta \approx 0.34\) and \(E\) an irreducible floor (the entropy of natural language — Topic 2; you cannot predict text better than its inherent randomness). Two ideas the exam wants you to internalize: loss is predictable in advance from small-scale fits (you extrapolate the power law and place your bet), and it has diminishing returns — each 10× of anything buys a fixed additive drop in loss, never a fixed fractional one.

⚛ Physics bridge

A power law with no characteristic scale is the signature of scale invariance — the same self-similarity you know from critical phenomena and renormalization. \(L - E \sim N^{-\alpha}\) is a straight line on a log–log plot, and the exponent \(\alpha\) is a critical exponent: a dimensionless number that characterizes the whole family of models, the way \(\nu\) or \(\gamma\) characterizes a universality class. That a $100M training run's loss can be read off a curve fit to $10k runs is exactly the physicist's faith that critical exponents measured small predict behavior large.

16.2 Chinchilla — how to spend a fixed compute budget

Here is the decision that made Chinchilla famous. Compute is fixed at \(C \approx 6ND\) (Topic 12). You can spend it on a bigger model trained on less data, or a smaller model trained on more data. Minimize \(L(N, D)\) subject to \(6ND = C\), and the optimum has a strikingly simple form: scale \(N\) and \(D\) in equal proportion — every doubling of compute should go half to a bigger model, half to more tokens. Empirically this lands near $$D_{\text{opt}} \approx 20\,N_{\text{opt}},$$ about 20 tokens per parameter. GPT-3 (175B params, 300B tokens ⇒ ~1.7 tokens/param) was massively over-parameterized and under-trained; Chinchilla (70B params, 1.4T tokens) used the same compute, obeyed the rule, and beat it — the single most cited "we were doing it wrong" result in the field.

The inference-aware correction — where Level III diverges from the formula

Chinchilla minimizes training loss per training FLOP. But a deployed model's compute is dominated by inference, run billions of times. A smaller model that is "over-trained" past the Chinchilla point (LLaMA-style: 7B params on 1–2T+ tokens, far past 20×) costs more to train but is permanently cheaper to serve. So the compute-optimal answer and the deployment-optimal answer differ — and choosing between them, given your expected inference volume, is exactly the kind of judgment call Level III grades. The formula is the start of the argument, not the end.

16.3 Mixture-of-Experts — decoupling capacity from cost

Scaling laws reward more parameters, but every parameter in a dense model runs on every token, so cost tracks capacity one-to-one. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) breaks that coupling. Replace each dense feed-forward block with \(E\) parallel "expert" FFNs plus a small router that, per token, sends it to only the top-\(k\) experts (typically \(k=1\) or 2). A model can then hold, say, 8× the parameters while activating the same FLOPs per token — sparse activation. Mixtral, GPT-4-class systems, and DeepSeek are MoE.

The price is engineering, not free lunch:

  • Load balancing. Left alone, the router collapses — a few popular experts get all the tokens (rich get richer), the rest starve, and you've paid for parameters you don't use. An auxiliary load-balancing loss penalizes uneven routing, pushing tokens toward a uniform spread. Watch this failure and its fix in the widget.
  • Capacity factor & token dropping. Experts run on fixed-size hardware buffers; if too many tokens route to one expert, the overflow is dropped (skips the layer). The capacity factor trades wasted compute (padding) against dropped tokens (lost computation).
  • Memory & communication. All experts must be stored (the full parameter count in memory even if sparsely used), and in distributed training the tokens must be all-to-all shuffled to their experts' devices and back — a communication pattern as demanding as Topic 12's tensor parallelism.

The exam-ready summary: MoE buys parameter capacity (hence quality) at fixed active FLOPs, paying in total memory, communication complexity, and training instability. It is the right tool when you are memory-and-bandwidth rich but latency-and-FLOP constrained.

Interactive — MoE routing and the load-balancing loss
A router sends a stream of tokens to 8 experts (top-1). Turn the load-balancing loss on and off, and watch expert utilization go from collapsed to balanced — and the "dropped token" rate respond to the capacity factor.

Things to try: (1) Loss weight 0: route a few batches and the distribution collapses — two or three experts hoard the tokens, the rest idle. That's the rich-get-richer instability every MoE paper warns about. (2) Raise the loss weight and reset: utilization flattens toward uniform. (3) At low capacity factor, the popular experts overflow and drop tokens (red) — those tokens skip the layer entirely; raising the factor cuts drops at the cost of padding compute.

16.4 Long context — and why it isn't free

Context length is a headline capability (whole codebases, book-length documents), but attention's cost is quadratic in sequence length \(s\): the score matrix is \(s \times s\). Doubling context quadruples attention compute and — for the KV-cache at inference (Topic 8) — doubles memory that must be held for every token generated. The architectural responses, each with a price:

  • Efficient attention: FlashAttention (Topic 8) removes the memory term by tiling but keeps the \(s^2\) compute; sparse/sliding-window attention (Longformer, Mistral) makes it linear by attending only locally, sacrificing some global reach.
  • Position encoding that extrapolates: RoPE (Topic 8) with interpolation or NTK-scaling lets a model trained at 4k generalize to 32k+ — cheaply, but with quality decay you must evaluate, not assume.
  • KV-cache compression: GQA/MQA (Topic 8), and quantizing or evicting cache entries — trading recall of distant tokens for memory.

And a capability caveat that belongs in any Level III answer: a large context window is not large context competence. "Lost in the middle" (Topic 14) and needle-in-a-haystack evals routinely show models with 200k windows using the middle 150k poorly. Advertised context length is a spec; usable context is an empirical result.

16.5 Designing and defending an architecture — the Level III move

A Level III answer never just names a technique — it justifies the choice against the stated constraints and acknowledges the tradeoff it accepts. The reasoning skeleton:

  1. Extract the binding constraint. Latency-bound? Memory-bound? Cost-per-query bound? Data-limited? The constraint selects the architecture; state it explicitly.
  2. Compute the budget. Use 6ND, the memory math (Topic 12), and the scaling law to turn the constraint into numbers — a defensible design shows arithmetic.
  3. Choose, and name the cost. "MoE for capacity at fixed active FLOPs, accepting the memory and all-to-all communication burden"; "over-train a small dense model past Chinchilla because inference volume dominates lifetime compute." The named tradeoff is what earns the marks.
  4. State how you'd validate. Which eval decides whether the bet paid off — before committing the full run.

The self-check below is written in this format on purpose: not "what is the answer" but "which reasoning survives contact with the constraints."

Jargon decoder
scaling law
Empirical power law: test loss ≈ E + A/N^α + B/D^β; predictable and extrapolable across orders of magnitude.
Chinchilla-optimal
The N, D split minimizing loss at fixed compute; roughly D ≈ 20N (equal scaling of params and tokens).
over-training
Deliberately training a smaller model past the Chinchilla point for cheaper inference; the deployment-optimal, not compute-optimal, choice.
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
Many expert FFNs + a router; each token uses only top-k. Big parameter count, small active FLOPs.
router / top-k
The small network choosing which experts a token visits; k=1–2 is typical.
load-balancing loss
Auxiliary penalty forcing even expert usage; without it the router collapses onto a few experts.
capacity factor
Sizing of per-expert token buffers; too low drops tokens, too high wastes compute on padding.
sparse activation
Only a fraction of parameters run per token — the property that decouples MoE capacity from cost.
all-to-all
The collective that ships tokens to their experts' devices and back; MoE's dominant communication cost.
irreducible loss (E)
The scaling-law floor — the entropy of the data; no model beats it.

Self-check

1. You have a fixed compute budget and want the lowest possible training loss. Someone proposes GPT-3's recipe: a very large model on relatively few tokens. Chinchilla says:

c. The headline Chinchilla result. The two power-law terms A/N^α and B/D^β are balanced at the optimum; GPT-3 over-spent on N and starved D. Note (d) is half-true — total compute sets a floor on achievable loss, but only the optimal split reaches it.

2. You'll serve a model to hundreds of millions of daily queries for years. Training compute is a one-time cost. The defensible allocation, versus strict Chinchilla-optimal:

a. The inference-aware correction, and a textbook Level III judgment: Chinchilla minimizes training loss per training FLOP, but the deployed cost function is dominated by inference. The shallow loss valley (widget point 1) makes over-training nearly free in quality. Naming why you deviate from the formula is the graded skill.

3. An MoE model's aggregate quality is disappointing; profiling shows 3 of 32 experts handle 70% of tokens while 12 experts see almost none. The root cause and fix:

d. The rich-get-richer routing instability, straight from the widget. Under-used experts are wasted capacity you paid to store. The load-balancing loss is the designed remedy; capacity factor governs whether overflow is dropped. Neither (a) nor (c) touches the routing distribution.

4. A vendor advertises a 1M-token context window as evidence the model "never forgets." As a chartered engineer reviewing the claim, you should:

b. §16.4's caveat and §14's "lost in the middle." The window is the size of the input the model accepts; whether it uses the middle is measured, not assumed. Demanding evidence over spec sheets is the Level III professional stance.
