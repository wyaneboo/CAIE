Capstone System Design

This is the exam's integrative core, and the one that most resembles the real job: you're handed a multi-page business scenario and must produce a written design — end to end, with justified tradeoffs, quantitative estimates, and a risk and rollout plan. There is no answer key. You're graded like a CFA Level III essay, on the quality and defensibility of your reasoning. Everything you learned across the twenty preceding topics converges here; the skill this page builds is the method for turning a vague brief into a defensible architecture — and the discipline of naming every tradeoff you accept.

What a capstone answer must cover
  • Requirements elicitation and constraint identification — including the unstated ones.
  • End-to-end architecture: data → training/selection → serving → monitoring.
  • Model and infrastructure selection with justified tradeoffs.
  • Cost, latency, and reliability analysis with quantitative estimates.
  • Risk, safety, and ethics assessment.
  • A staged rollout and evaluation plan.

21.1 The method — how to attack any capstone brief

The single most common failure is jumping to a solution before understanding the problem. Work the brief in this order, and show the work:

  1. Elicit requirements and surface the hidden constraints. What is the actual job-to-be-done? Then the constraints that decide everything: latency target, request volume and its shape (peaks), budget, accuracy/quality bar, privacy and regulatory regime, data availability, team size, and time-to-market. The unstated constraints are where marks are won — "the brief says healthcare, so HIPAA and human-in-the-loop are non-negotiable," "it says consumer chat, so p95 < 2 s or users leave."
  2. Identify the binding constraint. One usually dominates. Latency-bound problems and cost-bound problems and accuracy-bound problems have different optimal architectures; name which one you're solving before you draw a box.
  3. Choose the approach for the problem type. Is this a knowledge problem (→ RAG, Topic 14), a behavior/skill problem (→ fine-tuning, Topic 17), a well-scoped classification (→ maybe a small classical model, Topic 4, not an LLM at all)? The strongest answers sometimes reject the fancy option: "this is a structured extraction task at massive volume — a fine-tuned small model beats an LLM API on both cost and latency."
  4. Design end to end, and quantify. Data pipeline → model/infra selection → serving pattern → monitoring, with 6ND and memory math (Topic 12), cost/token and SLO math (Topic 20), and named numbers. Estimates with stated assumptions beat vague adjectives every time.
  5. Assess risk, safety, and ethics — then stage the rollout. What can go wrong (drift, injection, bias, cost blowup, provider dependency), how you'd detect it, and a staged plan (shadow → canary → GA, Topic 13) with monitored thresholds and a rollback trigger.
  6. State your tradeoffs explicitly. The graded sentence is always "I chose X over Y, accepting cost Z, because constraint C dominates." A design with no acknowledged tradeoffs reads as a design that didn't understand the problem.
The rubric, in one line

Graders reward: a correctly identified binding constraint, an architecture that follows from it, quantitative estimates with stated assumptions, explicitly named tradeoffs, and a risk/rollout plan. They penalize: unjustified technology choices ("we'll use an agent"), missing constraints (no privacy analysis on health data), hand- waved numbers, and one-word deploy/don't decisions. Note there is no reward for picking the "right" model — there isn't one; the reward is for the reasoning.

21.2 A worked capstone — reasoning made visible

Brief: A national bank wants an AI assistant for its 12M retail customers to answer questions about their own accounts and the bank's products, in-app, 24/7. Regulated environment; answers about a customer's data must be accurate and auditable; peak load ~3,000 requests/second; p95 latency budget ~2.5 s; a mistake that leaks another customer's data is a catastrophic, reportable incident. Design it.

Binding constraints (elicited): (1) correctness + auditability on personal financial data — this dominates; a plausible-but-wrong answer is worse than no answer. (2) privacy/isolation — customer A must never see customer B's data. (3) regulatory — audit trail, human escalation, explainability. (4) latency and cost at 3k rps are real but secondary to the above.

Approach and architecture: This is a knowledge + strict-grounding problem, not a behavior problem, so the core is RAG (Topic 14), not fine-tuning the facts in — knowledge is per-customer and changes constantly, and RAG gives citations for the audit trail. Per-customer data is fetched through the existing, permissioned account APIs at request time (never pre-embedded into a shared index — that's the isolation guarantee); product docs live in a vector index. A mid-size instruction-tuned model (Topic 17), possibly fine-tuned for the bank's tone and refusal behavior (behavior, legitimately) but not its facts. Strict grounding: answer only from retrieved context, cite sources, and refuse/escalate when confidence or grounding is low — a faithfulness gate (Topic 14) before any answer ships.

Quantify: 3k rps × ~2.5 s budget ⇒ heavy use of batching and prefix-caching of the shared system prompt (Topic 20); estimate GPUs from throughput and cost/token; a fast fallback model and cached FAQ answers for graceful degradation. Risk/safety: prompt injection via product docs (treat as data, Topic 18/15); the data-leak failure is contained architecturally by per-request permissioned fetch + least privilege, not by trusting the model. Rollout: shadow on real traffic (log, don't answer) → employee dogfood → canary to 1% behind groundedness/latency/complaint guardrails → GA, with a kill-switch and a human-escalation path throughout. Named tradeoff: "RAG over fine-tuning the facts, accepting retrieval-stack complexity and latency, because auditability and freshness dominate and fine-tuning offers neither citations nor per-customer isolation."

Notice what made that answer strong: the constraint drove the architecture, every choice named its cost, and the catastrophic failure was contained by system design rather than model behavior. Now build one yourself.

Interactive — capstone design studio
Pick a brief, then make the six core decisions. The rubric scores your design the way a grader would — on fit to the binding constraint and named tradeoffs, not on a single "right" answer. Aim for a coherent, defensible whole.

Things to try: (1) Deliberately over-engineer — pick a big agent and fine-tune everything for the cost-bound summarization brief — and watch the rubric dock you for a constraint mismatch and unnamed cost. (2) Match the approach to the binding constraint (RAG for the knowledge brief, a small fine-tuned model for the high-volume extraction one) and the score climbs. (3) There's no single perfect answer — several coherent designs score well. The feedback explains why, which is the exam skill.

21.3 Common capstone traps

  • Solution-first. Naming a technology (agent, RAG, fine-tune) before the constraint that justifies it. The order is problem → constraint → approach, always.
  • Reaching for the biggest hammer. An LLM agent for a job a fine-tuned classifier or even a rules engine does better, cheaper, and more reliably. Sophistication is not the goal; fit is.
  • Ignoring the unstated constraints. No privacy analysis on personal data, no latency budget on a consumer product, no cost estimate at scale — the silent constraints are usually the binding ones.
  • Hand-waved numbers. "It'll be fast and cheap" earns nothing; "≈X GPUs at Y utilization ⇒ $Z/month, p95 ≈ W ms" earns the marks, even if your assumptions are rough — state them.
  • No tradeoffs. A design where everything is optimal is a design that didn't engage the constraints. Every real choice costs something; say what.
  • No rollout or rollback. "Then we deploy it" ignores the entire staged-delivery and reliability apparatus (Topics 13, 20). The plan to ship safely is part of the design.

Self-check

1. A capstone brief describes a high-volume, well-defined task: classify 50M incoming support emails/day into 20 categories, p99 < 100 ms, tight budget. A strong answer likely:

b. Matching approach to problem type and binding constraint. A bounded classification at 50M/day with a 100 ms budget is exactly where a small fine-tuned model wins on both axes. Reaching for the LLM agent (a, c) is the "biggest hammer" trap; RAG (d) solves a knowledge problem this isn't.

2. Two candidates design the bank assistant. Candidate A writes "we'll use RAG and a good model." Candidate B writes "RAG over fine-tuning because knowledge is per-customer, fresh, and needs citations for audit — accepting retrieval-stack complexity — with per-request permissioned fetch to guarantee isolation." B scores higher mainly because:

d. Both chose RAG; only B earned it. The graded content is the chain constraint → choice → named tradeoff, plus the architectural isolation guarantee. This is the entire difference between a pass and a strong pass at Level III.

3. Your capstone design is elegant but you've written nothing about what happens after "deploy." The clearest gap a grader will flag:

a. "Then we deploy" discards Topics 13 and 20 entirely. A complete answer includes detection, staged delivery with monitored thresholds, and a rollback trigger — the operational half of system design that separates a demo from a production plan.

4. Mid-design you realize two constraints conflict: the p95 latency budget pushes toward a small model, but the accuracy bar pushes toward a large one. The Level III move is to:

c. Constraint conflicts are the point of the exercise, not a failure of the brief. Naming the tension and resolving it with a concrete mechanism (routing, cascades, speculative decoding, caching) plus a validation plan is precisely the reasoning being graded. Ignoring either constraint (a, b) or giving up (d) forfeits the question.
