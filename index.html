How to use this course
  • Go in order. Each level assumes the previous one. Inside a level, topics are sequenced so jargon is defined before it's used.
  • Play with every widget. The interactive demos are not decorations — each one is built around a specific exam-style intuition. Drag the sliders until the behavior stops surprising you.
  • Treat each LOS box as an exam question. If you can't answer it cold, you haven't learned it. Every topic ends with a self-check quiz.
  • Follow the "Go deeper" links. Each page cites specific chapters of free textbooks and primary papers. The course is the map; those are the territory.
  • Track progress. Mark topics complete at the bottom of each page (stored locally in your browser).
⚛ Why physicists make good AI engineers

Machine learning is applied statistical mechanics with a job title. Gradient descent is overdamped motion in a potential; softmax is a Boltzmann distribution; cross-entropy loss is free energy; diffusion models literally run Langevin dynamics in reverse. Throughout the course, boxes like this one map new concepts onto physics you already own. The genuinely new material — and the part that separates seniors from juniors — is the engineering: how real systems are built, shipped, monitored, and kept safe. That's where levels II and III live.

Note on equations

Math is rendered with KaTeX from a CDN. If you're offline, you'll see raw LaTeX — which, as a physics graduate, you can read anyway. To serve the course locally, run python -m http.server in the repo folder and open http://localhost:8000 (opening files directly also works in most browsers).

The path from beginner to senior

The three levels mirror a real career arc. Level I is what a strong junior knows: the math, the statistics, the classical models, enough software engineering not to be dangerous. Level II is what a mid-level engineer does: build, train, deploy, and debug real systems — transformers, RAG pipelines, distributed training, production monitoring. Level III is what makes someone senior: judgment. Scaling-law arithmetic, alignment tradeoffs, agent reliability, inference economics, and the ability to defend — in writing — a decision made under uncertainty, including the decision not to build.

Total honest effort: about a thousand hours. There is no shortcut, but there is a well-lit path. Start with Topic 1.
